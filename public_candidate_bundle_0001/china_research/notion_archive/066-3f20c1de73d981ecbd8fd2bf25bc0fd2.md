## 1. Phosphogypsum plus SWRO reject brine: the two requested double salts
### What was actually demonstrated
**\[MEASURED\]** Oubelhas *et al.* report a phase-diagram-guided laboratory route that combined **MgSO₄·7H₂O extracted from reject brine** with **(NH₄)₂SO₄ and/or K₂SO₄ produced from phosphogypsum** to crystallise `(NH4)2Mg(SO4)2·6H2O` (NMgS, boussingaultite) and `(KNH4)Mg(SO4)2·6H2O` (KNMgS). The reject-brine sample came from an RO plant near Laayoune, Morocco, and the phosphogypsum came from the Jorf Lasfar complex, Morocco. **Source:** \[S1\].
**\[MEASURED\]** The study reports X-ray diffraction identification of its intermediate salts and laboratory crystallisation/precipitation guided by ternary/quaternary phase diagrams. This is **laboratory-scale chemical-process evidence**, not a reported pilot or commercial production campaign. **Source:** \[S1\].
**\[REPORTED\]** The authors describe this as the first approach combining those two waste streams for these two fertiliser salts. This is an author claim, not independent commercial validation. **Source:** \[S1\].
**\[MEASURED\]** The same research group’s 2024 paper concerns the different salt `K2Mg(SO4)2·6H2O`, not either requested ammonium-containing salt. It should not be used as commercial evidence for NMgS or KNMgS. **Source:** \[S13\].

## 2. Selective electrodialysis coupled with RO for wastewater nutrients
### The quoted recoveries and their limits
**\[MEASURED\]** Xu *et al.* report an SED–RO process recovery of **95.1% phosphate**, **91.4% ammonium**, **90.4% potassium**, **88.6% magnesium**, **94.7% sulfate** and **87.1% calcium**. **Source:** \[S2\].
**\[MEASURED\]** Those results were obtained with **synthetic swine wastewater prepared from analytical-reagent chemicals in deionised water**. They are not measurements on Dongjiakou municipal/industrial wastewater, food-park wastewater, chemical-park wastewater, desalination brine, or a mixed stream. **Source:** \[S2\].
**\[MEASURED\]** In the reported experimental routing, mixed streams and product streams were subsequently used for struvite; ammonium sulfate and mixed KCl/NaCl salts; and hydroxyapatite. The quoted ion recoveries are not a single finished fertiliser grade. **Source:** \[S2\].
**\[MEASURED\]** The primary study identifies water splitting, high energy at deep desalination, and the slower migration of phosphate as process-design constraints; it selected a 50–70% initial phosphate-desalination ratio to manage those effects. **Source:** \[S2\].
### Product quality and deployment caution
**\[REPORTED\]** A 2024 peer-reviewed review concludes that non-target ions migrate with recovered nutrients; sodium can accumulate with ammonium/potassium, while chloride/sulfate can accompany phosphate. It states that the resulting impurity burden can prevent direct fertiliser use without downstream purification. **Source:** \[S5\].
**\[REPORTED\]** The same review reports approximate ideal-condition energy demands of 5 kWh/kg for N and up to 80 kWh/kg for P recovery, and concludes that most relevant studies remain short-term bench-scale with rare economic-feasibility data. These are review-level reported values, not a Dongjiakou power demand or cost. **Source:** \[S5\].
**\[MODELED\]** The exact SED–RO system is assigned **TRL 3** because its cited high recoveries are from synthetic-feed laboratory work; a different ED pilot does not demonstrate this exact multi-ion SED–RO configuration on Dongjiakou feed. **Source:** \[S2\] \[S5\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No production cost per tonne of recovered P, N, K, Mg, struvite, ammonium sulfate, KCl-containing salt, or hydroxyapatite was reported for this exact SED–RO route. It would be invalid to convert recovery percentages into cost without feed concentration, electricity consumption by ion/product, membrane life, chemical consumption, waste management, capex and product-quality data. **Source:** \[S2\] \[S5\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No commercial-scale integrated SED–RO nutrient-recovery plant was identified in the reviewed evidence. The review’s statement that more long-term pilot work is required is evidence against treating the laboratory result as commercial readiness, but it is not an exhaustive global plant census. **Source:** \[S5\].
### Relevance to Dongjiakou
**\[MODELED\]** This pathway connects to wastewater rather than SWRO brine. It can become a fertiliser-feedstock option only if the relevant wastewater streams have sufficiently high recoverable nutrient concentrations and controlled contaminants; neither condition is evidenced in the supplied Dongjiakou data. **Source:** synthetic-feed limitation \[S2\]; no suitable Dongjiakou wastewater assay source found.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Annual P, N, K and Mg tonnage from Dongjiakou wastewater is unknown. The report has no annual wastewater volume, dissolved nutrient concentrations, fraction of each ion available after pretreatment, contamination profile, or demonstrated product yield. **Source:** no suitable source found; required recovery definitions \[S2\].
## 3. Fertiliser-drawn forward osmosis using ammonium sulfate
### What FDFO does—and does not—do
**\[MEASURED\]** El Zayat *et al.* tested industrial-grade `(NH4)2SO4` as a draw solution against **synthetic NaCl brine** representing RO brine. The work included bench-scale and pilot-scale investigations in batch mode. **Source:** \[S3\].
**\[MEASURED\]** The study reports an average water flux of **8.01 L m⁻² h⁻¹** and a brine-volume reduction of **12.4%**. In its pilot case, 5.75 m³ of 65 g/L brine was reduced to 5.00 m³ at 73 g/L using 0.29 m³ of 740 g/L initial draw solution. **Source:** \[S3\].
**\[MEASURED\]** A laboratory analysis of the final draw solution found 1,200 mg/L N; the paper’s illustrative scenario then added 5 m³ of RO permeate to reduce this to 205 mg/L N for fertigation. This is a dilute fertilised-water result, not a dry fertiliser grade or an N recovery from brine. **Source:** \[S3\].
**\[MODELED\]** FDFO creates a potential use for **imported or otherwise supplied ammonium sulfate** by diluting it with water extracted from brine. It does not recover N, P, K or Mg molecules from a NaCl-dominated SWRO brine. Therefore it cannot by itself improve strategic nutrient self-sufficiency. **Source:** process inputs/results \[S3\].
### Laboratory/pilot versus commercial evidence
**\[MEASURED\]** A separate pilot-scale FDFO–nanofiltration field trial ran for about six months on Australian saline coal-mine water, not SWRO brine. The authors report that reverse diffusion left the FO feed brine above effluent-discharge limits for ammonium and sulfate, and that Na/Cl buildup could affect final fertiliser-water quality. **Source:** \[S4\].
**\[MODELED\]** For the narrow use case “ammonium sulfate draw to concentrate SWRO brine,” **TRL 4–5** is the defensible range: an integrated bench/pilot investigation exists, but its feed was synthetic and no continuous commercial SWRO installation was located. The range, rather than a single TRL, reflects the lack of a published pilot throughput and duration for the synthetic-brine study. **Source:** \[S3\] \[S4\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Production cost per tonne of N, ammonium sulfate, fertilised-water product, or brine avoided is not reported in the exact ammonium-sulfate/synthetic-brine study. A separate techno-economic FDFO study concerns urban wastewater/greenwall fertigation and reports conditions for water-cost competitiveness, not a transferable cost per tonne of nutrient for SWRO brine. **Source:** \[S3\] \[S15\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No commercial-scale FDFO plant treating SWRO reject brine with ammonium sulfate as the draw was identified in the reviewed literature. **Source:** \[S3\] \[S4\] \[S15\].
### Literature tension that matters
**\[MEASURED\]** The synthetic-brine study demonstrates modest brine concentration and a fertigation-oriented diluted draw solution. **Source:** \[S3\].
**\[MEASURED\]** The real mine-water pilot demonstrates sustained field operation but reports reverse solute diffusion and salt accumulation risks. **Source:** \[S4\].
**\[MODELED\]** These findings are not contradictory: they show that FDFO can transfer water across the membrane, while also showing that nutrient/salt crossover and final-water quality can limit a closed-loop fertiliser application. They do not establish viable SWRO-brine nutrient recovery or commercial economics. **Source:** \[S3\] \[S4\].
## 4. China GB suitability and agricultural-market pathway
### Standards and registration
**\[REPORTED\]** GB/T 20937-2018, *Potassium magnesium of sulphate fertilizer*, is current. Its published scope is potassium-magnesium sulfate fertiliser made from salt-lake brine or solid potassium-magnesium salt ore by physical extraction/direct impurity removal, and it expressly excludes products blended from potassium sulfate and magnesium compounds. **Source:** \[S7\] \[S16\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Neither NMgS nor KNMgS can be said to comply with GB/T 20937-2018. The laboratory route uses phosphogypsum chemical conversion and seawater reject brine rather than the stated feed/material route; moreover, no evidence was found of a finished-product test against the standard’s requirements. This is a scope-and-evidence conclusion, not a finding that the salts necessarily fail every quality limit. **Source:** \[S1\] \[S16\].
**\[REPORTED\]** China’s official Fertilizer Registration Management Measures require registration before a fertiliser may be imported, produced, sold or used unless exempt; they specifically require MARA record-filing for agricultural potassium-magnesium sulfate. The exemption list includes ammonium sulfate, KCl and potassium sulfate, but does not name the two requested double salts. **Source:** \[S8\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** A novel NMgS or KNMgS product’s precise Chinese regulatory category is unknown until MARA or a competent authority classifies the formulation. On the available evidence it should **not** be treated as automatically exempt: the Measures require chemical, efficacy, safety and label materials for registration, and field trials are generally required before registration unless relief applies. **Source:** \[S8\].
**\[REPORTED\]** The Chinese standards inventory includes GB/T 535-2020 for fertiliser-grade ammonium sulfate, GB/T 37918-2019 for fertiliser-grade KCl, GB/T 20937-2018 for potassium-magnesium sulfate, and GB 38400-2019 for limits on toxic and harmful substances in fertilisers. Listing a standard does not certify a product. **Source:** \[S6\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The SED–RO products and FDFO fertigation solution lack reported Chinese-standard assays, product labels, MARA filing/registration records, field efficacy evidence and contaminant/radionuclide data. Their GB suitability is therefore unknown. **Source:** \[S2\] \[S3\] \[S8\].
### Policy alignment—not a subsidy proof for fertiliser
**\[REPORTED\]** The NDRC/Natural Resources 2021 desalination action plan calls for resource utilisation of concentrated brine, including aquaculture and salt-tolerant crops, and provides policy support for desalination; it does not establish a dedicated fertiliser subsidy or a product-approval route for brine-derived double salts. **Source:** \[S9\].
**\[REPORTED\]** The 2025 National Seawater Utilization Report says Chinese seawater chemical-resource products include bromine, KCl, MgCl₂, MgSO₄ and K₂SO₄, and that Tianjin, Hebei and Shandong are pursuing brine salt/bromine/potassium research and industrialisation. It documents a brine-to-bromine project at Tangshan, not a brine-to-fertiliser facility. **Source:** \[S10\].
**\[REPORTED\]** The same official report states Qingdao’s policy offers one-time support for new desalination projects based on capacity and comprehensive-utilisation performance: 10% of fixed-asset investment for non-island projects and 20% for island projects, capped at CNY 10 million. This is project support, not evidence that a fertiliser product will be registered, profitable or eligible. **Source:** \[S10\].
## 5. Potash market comparator: what can and cannot be compared
**\[REPORTED\]** Mysteel reports that China’s 2025 annual potash import contract was agreed at **US\$346/t CFR** on 12 June 2025. **Source:** \[S11\].
**\[REPORTED\]** SunSirs reported on 26 January 2026 a later annual “big contract” at **US\$348/t**, domestic 60% white potash at **CNY 3,300/t**, border-trade 62% white potash at **CNY 3,400/t**, port 62% white potash at **CNY 3,500/t**, and its KCl benchmark at **CNY 3,583.33/t**. This is a secondary commodity-platform report and should be treated as reported market intelligence, not audited transaction data. **Source:** \[S12\].
**\[MODELED\]** KCl price cannot be compared directly with NMgS because NMgS has zero potassium. KNMgS carries only 12.341% K₂O-equivalent by formula; therefore 4.862 t pure KNMgS contains the K₂O in one tonne of 60%-K₂O KCl. Formula: `0.600 / 0.123408 = 4.862`. This ignores availability, nutrient-response equivalence, logistics, product quality, tax, registration, and all costs. **Source:** KCl grade basis reported in \[S12\]; KNMgS formula \[S1\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** There is no evidence-based comparison of pathway production cost against US\$346–348/t CFR KCl or CNY 3,300–3,583.33/t domestic KCl because no pathway unit cost was reported. A positive or negative economic conclusion would be fabricated. **Source:** \[S1\] \[S2\] \[S3\] \[S11\] \[S12\].
## 6. Dongjiakou mass balance: answer constrained by missing chemistry
### What can be calculated now
**\[MODELED\]** For any dissolved nutrient `i`, annual recoverable nutrient mass is:
`M_i (t/year) = V_brine (m³/year) × C_i (mg/L) × R_i × Y_product / 1,000,000`
where `V_brine` is 18–22 million m³/year from the project context, `C_i` is a representative measured dissolved concentration, `R_i` is demonstrated recovery to the intended product stream, and `Y_product` is the product-conversion yield after purification/crystallisation. **Source:** volume \[S14\]; recovery/product-stream distinction \[S1\] \[S2\] \[S3\].
**\[MODELED\]** For pure double-salt stoichiometry, 1.000 t of Mg in feed could form at most 14.836 t NMgS or 15.702 t KNMgS, if Mg were 100% recovered and all other reactants were available. For KNMgS, 1.000 t K could form at most 9.761 t product and requires 0.622 t Mg; potassium will generally set the salt’s stoichiometric ceiling when K/Mg is low. These are molecular-ratio ceilings, not plant yields. **Source:** formulae \[S1\].
**\[MODELED\]** For SED–RO, the published recovery fractions can only be applied after measuring the appropriate wastewater feed, not the SWRO brine: `M_P = V_wastewater × C_P × 0.951 × product yield`, with parallel equations for NH₄⁺, K⁺ and Mg²⁺. **Source:** recovery fractions \[S2\].
### What cannot be calculated now
**\[UNKNOWN\]** **Actual Dongjiakou annual tonnes of K, Mg, N, P, fertiliser salt or K₂O-equivalent from 18–22 million m³/year of brine are unknown.** The required brine assays for K, Mg, sulfate, calcium, sodium, chloride, boron, trace metals and organics; temporal variability; effective membrane rejection; mineral-extraction yield; and purge losses were not supplied or found. Using generic seawater composition, another plant’s brine, or 45–50% RO recovery as a substitute would be an estimate prohibited by this evidence register. **Source:** project flow \[S14\]; feed-specific laboratory nature of \[S1\]–\[S3\].
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Actual wastewater-derived P/N/K/Mg output is likewise unknown: no Dongjiakou wastewater volume, dissolved-ion concentrations, solids content, competing salts, or product purity data were supplied. **Source:** \[S2\]; no suitable Dongjiakou wastewater assay source found.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** FDFO has no brine-derived nutrient mass to count. Its nutrient inventory is ammonium sulfate deliberately added as draw solution; the recoverable output relevant to brine is water transfer/volume reduction, not new N/P/K/Mg. **Source:** \[S3\].
