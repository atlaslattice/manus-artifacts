## Executive summary
- **\[REPORTED\] Wastewater surveillance has repeatedly detected and geographically tracked poliovirus circulation before or without clinical paralysis, and has informed vaccination responses; it has *not*, by itself, eliminated a pathogen.** Israel’s 2013 wild-poliovirus outbreak is the clearest named case: sewage detection triggered an emergency response and a vaccination campaign, after which circulation subsided. Causation is observational; a transmission model estimated that the campaign averted infections, not that sampling did. \[S04\] \[S05\] \[S06\]
- **\[MEASURED\] For poliovirus, a sewer catchment of roughly 100,000–300,000 people is recommended for routine environmental surveillance; larger catchments dilute rare shedding, and a wastewater positive cannot yield a reliable infected-person count.** The minimum reliably detectable number of infected people is explicitly unknown. This number cannot be transferred to other pathogens or to Dongjiakou without a sewer map and validation. \[S02\] \[S03\]
- **\[REPORTED\] Wastewater can detect genetic targets from enteric and respiratory viruses, bacteria, protozoa and antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes, but capability is pathogen-, matrix-, sewer- and method-specific.** It is strongest for targets that are shed, stable and have validated assays. A negative sample is not proof of absence. \[S01\] \[S03\] \[S17\]
- **\[MEASURED\] Sequencing can distinguish strains and variants and metagenomics can survey many genes at once, including AMR genes; it is not a dependable “unknown pathogen detector.”** In a global sewage metagenomic study, 68% of reads could not be assigned to a reference sequence, while only 0.03% mapped to AMR genes. Direct sequencing for wastewater remains comparatively immature and may be less sensitive than targeted assays. \[S01\] \[S09\]
- **\[MEASURED\] AMR wastewater measurements correlate reasonably well with matched human AMR prevalence in a systematic review (phenotypic concordance coefficient 0.85, 95% CI 0.80–0.89; genotypic 0.88, 95% CI 0.84–0.90), but the evidence base was rated low-to-moderate certainty and does not establish patient-level diagnosis or prescribe antibiotics.** No verified case was found in which locality wastewater AMR data changed routine clinical prescribing and improved patient outcomes. \[S08\]
- **\[MODELED\] A locality programme’s cost and staffing cannot be credibly priced for Dongjiakou from public evidence.** A U.S. military model assumed six labour-hours per wastewater test and once-weekly testing; it is a context-specific model, not an observed Chinese municipal budget. Sampling, laboratory quality control, bioinformatics, epidemiology and communications all require sustained personnel. \[S11\] \[S10\]
- **\[REPORTED\] China’s 2024 national policy directs national/provincial laboratory and environmental-risk networks and allows monitoring of pathogens in urban and aircraft wastewater when needed.** A Shenzhen project demonstrates Chinese city-level development activity, but no public, outcome-verified wastewater pathogen programme specific to Qingdao or Dongjiakou was identified in targeted Chinese- and English-language searches. \[S13\] \[S14\]
- **\[REPORTED\] Wastewater data should remain a public-health signal, not a policing or worker-discipline tool.** Governance needs purpose limitation, minimum catchment size, independent public-health/ethics oversight, transparent triggers, secure data sharing, community benefit and a firewall against law-enforcement use. \[S10\] \[S16\]
## Bottom-line answer
**\[REPORTED\] Detection, tracking and elimination are different claims.** Detection is a laboratory finding in an aggregate environmental sample. Tracking is repeated, spatially resolved and, where possible, sequenced measurement used to map a signal or its lineage. Elimination is interruption of transmission, demonstrated with sufficiently sensitive clinical and environmental surveillance over time and achieved by interventions such as vaccination, treatment, source control or sanitation—not by surveillance alone. \[S02\] \[S03\] \[S05\]
**Verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** A carefully governed wastewater component can support *detection* and *population-level tracking* at a locality, especially for faecally shed pathogens and AMR trends. Polio supplies strong real-world evidence that environmental detection can trigger and guide suppression activity. It does **not** support a claim that wastewater monitoring itself eliminates pathogens, that one sample represents a known number of people, or that broad “unknown pathogen” sequencing provides reliable early warning without prior validation. The appropriate claim at Dongjiakou is **“actionable environmental surveillance, integrated with clinical and occupational-health surveillance,”** not “pathogen elimination through monitoring.” \[S01\] \[S02\] \[S03\]

## What the evidence shows—and what it does not
### 1. Evidence that wastewater surveillance has contributed to suppression or elimination
#### The strongest named case: Israel’s 2013–14 silent wild-poliovirus type 1 outbreak
**\[REPORTED—observational outbreak evidence\]** Israel, certified polio-free in 2002, recovered wild poliovirus type 1 (WPV1) from routine sewage samples taken in two southern cities during 7–13 April 2013. Strain-specific analysis placed introduction in early February; the country began supplementary oral-polio vaccination in August 2013. \[S04\]
**\[REPORTED—observational surveillance evidence\]** The positive sewage finding triggered an emergency response. Intensified surveillance expanded to 74 sites and documented sustained high WPV1 viral load in six Bedouin settlements and two southern cities, with lower/intermittent signals in 14 mixed communities elsewhere. The investigators state that the strain-specific assay informed response and decision-making. \[S05\]
**\[REPORTED—observational outcome\]** No acute-flaccid-paralysis cases were reported in this outbreak, and the epidemic subsided after the bivalent oral-polio-vaccine campaign. This is compelling evidence that environmental surveillance detected silent circulation and guided the response; it is **not** a controlled experiment that isolates the effect of surveillance from vaccination, prior immunity, sanitation, behaviour or other response measures. \[S06\]
**\[MODELED—not directly measured\]** A mathematical reconstruction estimated basic reproduction number 1.62 (95% CI 1.04–2.02), estimated 59% (95% CI 9–77%) of susceptible people were infected over a little more than six months, and estimated the vaccination campaign averted 10% (95% CI 1–24%) of infections. Those figures quantify a model, not observed infections or the causal effect of sewage surveillance. \[S06\]
> **Evidence-register conclusion:** Israel is a named, well-documented case of wastewater/environmental surveillance contributing to *detection, geographic tracking and response-associated suppression*. It is **not** evidence that sampling eliminated WPV1, and it cannot establish that the campaign’s observed outcome was caused only by surveillance.
#### Polio eradication programme: powerful surveillance, not a surveillance-only causal claim
**\[REPORTED\]** The Global Polio Eradication Initiative uses environmental surveillance alongside acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance. WHO/GPEI guidance describes positive wild or vaccine-derived poliovirus in sewage as a trigger for immediate laboratory and epidemiological investigation, enhanced sampling, active case finding and, where appropriate, immunization response. \[S02\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO declared wild poliovirus types 2 and 3 eradicated in 2015 and 2019 respectively, as reported by CDC. That achievement should not be credited to wastewater surveillance alone: eradication depended principally on immunization, clinical surveillance, laboratory networks, outbreak response and containment. Wastewater surveillance is an adjunct that reduces the chance of missing asymptomatic circulation. \[S03\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The U.S. 2022–23 response after a paralytic vaccine-derived-polio case in New York illustrates *tracking*, not elimination: wastewater sequencing found a linked lineage across multiple New York counties and a second genetically distinct importation in New York City. The authors explicitly state that the value of routine poliovirus wastewater surveillance outside an outbreak remains unclear. \[S07\]
#### Limits that prevent an elimination claim
**\[REPORTED\]** Poliovirus in wastewater can indicate importation and, if repeatedly detected, possible person-to-person transmission. It cannot determine how many people are infected or what share of the population is infected; CDC states that the minimum reliably detectable number of infected people is unknown. \[S03\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A single environmental poliovirus detection can derive from one healthy importer and may not pose a transmission risk in a well-immunized population. GPEI therefore requires confirmatory laboratory, epidemiological and clinical investigation rather than treating one positive as proof of an outbreak. \[S02\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No controlled trial was identified in which localities were randomized to wastewater surveillance versus no wastewater surveillance and pathogen elimination was the outcome. For ethical and operational reasons, such evidence may be impractical; the causal chain is instead supported by outbreak case studies, routine surveillance experience, response logic and models.
### 2. Sensitivity, catchment size and what one sampling point represents
**\[REPORTED—polio-specific operational guidance\]** GPEI recommends a catchment of approximately **100,000–300,000 people** per poliovirus environmental-surveillance site as an operational optimum. At a larger site, more contributors can dilute rare shedding below detection; at a smaller site, too few excreters or too-short circulation may delay or prevent detection at cost-effective sampling frequencies. The guidance gives **1 CCID50 per 1.5 mL** wastewater as the minimum cell-culture requirement. \[S02\]
**\[REPORTED—polio-specific U.S. guidance\]** CDC recommends global poliovirus sewersheds of approximately 100,000–300,000 people and, for U.S. use, a catchment no larger than 400,000 where possible; a positive in a larger sewershed may warrant upstream sampling to narrow the population at risk. \[S03\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** There is no valid universal “population represented by one sample” number for SARS-CoV-2, influenza, norovirus, enteric bacteria, parasites or AMR genes. Sensitivity changes with shedding amount and timing, prevalence, sample volume and frequency, sewer travel time, dilution by stormwater and industry, inhibitor load, concentration method, extraction recovery, assay limit of detection, normalisation marker and the fraction of residents actually connected to the sampled flow. WHO describes the catchment-to-human contribution relationship as dynamic and complex. \[S01\]
**\[REPORTED\]** For any prospective Dongjiakou system, the relevant population is the people and institutions whose waste actually reaches a defined sampling point during the sampling window—not everyone who lives or works within an administrative boundary. An outlet blending municipal and industrial flow is an aggregate population-and-process signal; it is not a resident prevalence survey. \[S01\] \[S03\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The local industrial wastewater operator reports separate industrial treatment of 20,000 m³/day and municipal treatment of 5,000 m³/day in the Dongjiakou economic zone. That supports the existence of potential sampling interfaces but does not supply a pathogen-surveillance catchment population, sewer connectivity, flow variability, inhibitory chemistry or analytical recovery. \[S15\]
**Implication for design:** **\[UNKNOWN\]** Until sewer diagrams, treatment-train layout, industrial discharges, resident/worker connectivity, hydraulic residence time and assay recovery are measured, it is unknown whether one Dongjiakou treatment-plant influent sample could provide sensitive locality surveillance. A defensible pilot would validate a municipal influent sample and, only if justified, add upstream residential/worker-catchment sites; it must not infer individual buildings or persons from plant-level results.
### 3. Detectable pathogen range and laboratory approaches
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO says an environmental target must be shed into the sampled matrix, persist sufficiently to be detected, and have methods with adequate sensitivity and specificity for the intended decision. A high-burden pathogen without a response action is not automatically a good wastewater target. \[S01\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Documented wastewater analytical options include microscopy; culture and selective media; biochemical, serological and antimicrobial-susceptibility testing of isolates; cell-culture infectivity assays; immunological tests; conventional PCR, qPCR/RT-qPCR, digital PCR and droplet-digital PCR; whole-genome, 16S rRNA, targeted-amplicon and metagenomic sequencing. These are methods, not proof that every target can be measured reliably in every sewer. \[S01\]

**\[REPORTED\]** PCR is targeted and can be highly sensitive for the sequence selected, but it cannot ordinarily distinguish viable from nonviable cells or quantify infectious risk directly. Wastewater clinical assays often need matrix-specific optimisation because environmental samples have many organisms and inhibitors. \[S01\] \[S17\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Sequencing can identify variants, sequence types and AMR profiles. Targeted-amplicon or whole-genome approaches work best when a target or lineage is already known; sequencing data require bioinformatics and carefully curated reference databases. \[S01\]
**\[MEASURED\]** In a 79-site, 60-country untreated-sewage metagenomic study, average sequencing was 120 million reads/sample; on average, 0.03% of reads mapped to AMR genes and 68% could not be assigned to a reference sequence. The study shows broad resistome profiling is feasible but also why an “unassigned” sequence is not evidence of a new human pathogen. \[S09\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO characterises direct sequencing approaches in wastewater as still in their infancy for this application. Metagenomics can generate hypotheses and discover unexpected genetic material, but confirmation by targeted assay, culture where appropriate, clinical/animal/environmental investigation, and epidemiology remains necessary before declaring a novel pathogen event. \[S01\]
### 4. AMR surveillance: real evidence, but not clinical diagnosis
**\[MEASURED—systematic review\]** Chau et al. systematically reviewed 8,867 records, included 33 studies, and extracted AMR prevalence data from 24. They found reasonably high concordance between wastewater and human AMR prevalence: phenotypic CCC 0.85 (95% CI 0.80–0.89) and genotypic CCC 0.88 (95% CI 0.84–0.90). Composite influent sampling, more than 12 months of longitudinal sampling, and time/location-matched human and wastewater populations generally showed better agreement in descriptive synthesis. \[S08\]
**\[REPORTED—review limitation\]** The systematic review rated certainty **low to moderate**, because studies were heterogeneous, small and inconsistently reported. It found no specific detection method clearly superior through its meta-regression. The results support population trend surveillance—not replacement of clinical microbiology. \[S08\]
**\[MEASURED\]** Global sewage metagenomics found systematic regional differences in AMR-gene abundance/diversity across 79 sites in 60 countries. It measured a community resistome, not the prevalence of resistant infections in patients; its authors note one sample per site and call for comparisons of sensitivity and specificity among methods. \[S09\]
**\[UNKNOWN—documented null finding\]** No controlled study, prospective locality programme, or official clinical guideline was identified in targeted searches that showed wastewater AMR results changed empiric antibiotic choice for individual patients, reduced inappropriate prescribing, or improved patient outcomes. The reviewed evidence concerns concordance and surveillance potential, not clinical action efficacy. \[S08\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The cautious use case is to flag a trend or an unusual resistance determinant, then trigger confirmatory clinical/occupational/animal-health sampling and infection-control review. It is not to infer that any person, workforce, neighbourhood or facility is infected with a resistant pathogen. \[S08\] \[S10\]
### 5. China and the Dongjiakou/Qingdao setting
**\[REPORTED—national policy\]** China’s National Disease Control and Prevention Administration’s 2024 guidance calls for a national and provincial pathogen-microorganism laboratory monitoring network; national/provincial vector, host-animal and environmental-risk monitoring sites; multi-sectoral information sharing; and, as needed, monitoring emerging/imported infectious-disease pathogens in urban and inbound-aircraft wastewater. It also calls for provincial and capable municipal CDCs to develop high-throughput multi-pathogen testing, metagenomics and bioinformatics capacity. This is a policy commitment and capacity direction, not proof that every province/city operates a mature wastewater programme. \[S13\]
**\[REPORTED—local Chinese development example\]** Shenzhen CDC announced in March 2024 a research-and-demonstration project intended to develop methods for five pathogens and a city-level wastewater-pipe-network warning system. The announcement describes project launch and intended outputs, not measured health outcomes or a validated national standard. \[S14\]
**\[UNKNOWN—documented null finding\]** Targeted searches in Chinese and English did not identify a publicly documented, routine pathogen-wastewater surveillance programme, published pathogen results, a validated catchment map, staffing plan or budget specific to **Qingdao Dongjiakou**. The absence of a public record is not evidence that no internal monitoring occurs.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** It is unknown whether Dongjiakou’s municipal sewage fraction is large, stable or sufficiently separated from industrial wastewater to support pathogen trend interpretation. It is also unknown whether Qingdao CDC, the treatment-plant operator, port-health authorities and local hospitals have a data-sharing/response protocol for a positive environmental signal.
### 6. Costs, staffing and an honest locality-scale programme specification
**\[MODELED\]** The published U.S. Air Force cost model assumed once-weekly testing in its baseline scenario, six labour-hours per wastewater test, material cost of US\$54.41 per wastewater test, and per-site weekly costs for surveillance/data management/reporting, support, vendor management and programme oversight. At 82 bases, it estimated US\$5.4 million/year for once-weekly monitoring. These are model inputs/outputs in U.S. military operations, not directly observed costs and not transferable as a Dongjiakou quote. \[S11\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The National Academies finds that a sustainable programme needs capacity from site selection and sampling through laboratory analysis, data analysis, interpretation and communication. It calls for predictable funding and close coordination among public-health agencies, analytical laboratories and wastewater utilities. \[S10\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** A credible annual capital or operating cost in CNY, full-time-equivalent staffing number, sampling frequency, turnaround time and per-sample price for Dongjiakou cannot be calculated from the available public evidence. No estimate is supplied here.

### 7. Air and surface alternatives: comparison, not substitution
**\[MEASURED—single-building comparative study\]** In one university-building SARS-CoV-2 study, wastewater had the lowest putative false-negative rate (0%) and highest agreement with clinical surveillance (83%) among wastewater, rooftop-exhaust aerosol and surface methods. This is a site-specific comparison using a known clinical census; it cannot establish universal sensitivity or performance for Dongjiakou. \[S12\]
**\[MEASURED\]** In that same study, wastewater had less spatial resolution: it identified a wing-scale signal but not the room/building source. Rooftop exhaust air sampling could resolve room groups connected to one exhaust stack, although HVAC mixing, open windows and fresh-air flows could generate apparently false positive/negative signals. \[S12\]
**\[MEASURED\]** High-touch surface samples had the most putative false negatives, and cleaning can both reduce signal and leave residual nucleic acid that complicates “current infection” interpretation. \[S12\]

### 8. Governance, data sharing and surveillance-overreach safeguards
**\[REPORTED\]** National Academies guidance recommends governance with public-health, community, statistical and ethics expertise; data-use agreements for secondary users; and assessment against proportionality, equity and transparency. It specifically recommends a strong firewall against law-enforcement use. \[S10\]
**\[REPORTED\]** GAO identifies privacy risk from pathogen and human genetic data, potential stigma where results are associated with communities or drug use, method standardisation problems, and unclear cost-benefit evidence. These are not hypothetical reasons to abandon surveillance; they are reasons to restrict use to legitimate, demonstrably beneficial public-health purposes. \[S16\]
**\[REPORTED\]** China’s 2024 policy requires cross-sector monitoring, information sharing and cybersecurity/data-security support for monitoring-warning platforms. It does not, in the cited policy text, specify a Dongjiakou-level protocol for granular wastewater results, industrial data, community notice, secondary research access or a law-enforcement firewall. \[S13\]
**Governance conditions before collection or publication:**
- **\[REPORTED\]** Establish a written public-health purpose, target list, action thresholds, response owner and sunset/review criteria before sampling. WHO prioritises targets that are actionable and add value to existing surveillance. \[S01\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** Use the least granular site capable of achieving the public-health purpose. At small catchments, building-level data can increase re-identification and stigma risk; do not name a workplace, dormitory, vessel or neighbourhood absent a necessary, proportionate response pathway. \[S10\] \[S16\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** Share confirmed, quality-controlled information with CDC/public-health authorities, utilities and named response partners under role-based access and documented data-use agreements. Public communication should distinguish wastewater detection from identified infected people. \[S03\] \[S10\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** Include community/worker/resident representation in oversight and publish methods, uncertainty, catchment meaning and response actions—not raw alarms alone. \[S10\]
- **\[UNKNOWN\]** Whether China’s applicable laws, local health authority procedures and industrial-park governance permit each proposed data flow requires local legal and institutional review; this report does not provide legal advice.
### 9. A bounded, evidence-aligned value proposition for Dongjiakou
**\[REPORTED—conditional mechanism\]** A pilot could produce public-health value if it starts with a small, action-linked panel rather than indiscriminate sequencing: one or more validated faecally shed pathogen targets selected by Qingdao CDC, a limited AMR trend panel, process controls, and predefined escalation to clinical confirmation. Its legitimate benefits would be earlier aggregate signals, variant/lineage or resistance-trend context, and more equitable coverage of people who do not present for testing. \[S01\] \[S03\] \[S08\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** It is not known whether such a pilot will detect events earlier than local clinical systems, save money, reduce illness, prevent antimicrobial-resistant infection, improve quality of life or avoid ecological harms at Dongjiakou. Those outcomes require an evaluation protocol with baseline measures and a counterfactual or otherwise credible comparison.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No evidence supporting claims that wastewater surveillance will eliminate disease, identify infected individuals, prove an outbreak from one result, give precise infection prevalence, identify an unknown pathogen reliably by default, or direct individual antibiotic treatment was identified.

### Evidence-quality note
**\[REPORTED\]** The strongest operational evidence here is the mature poliovirus surveillance system and named outbreak records. AMR has a systematic review with quantitative concordance but low-to-moderate certainty and no clinical-outcome evidence. Multi-pathogen wastewater surveillance is rapidly developing; WHO’s 2024 guidance is explicitly a pilot version and lists method validation, interpretation, ethics, workforce and cost-effectiveness as research needs. The cost and airborne/surface comparison evidence is context-specific. \[S01\] \[S08\] \[S10\]
