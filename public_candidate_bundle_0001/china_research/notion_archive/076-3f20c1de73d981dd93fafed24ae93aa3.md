## Executive summary
- **MEASURED — comparative and long-term roof monitoring.** Vegetated roofs reliably reduce **roof heat exposure** and **small-to-moderate storm runoff** at the building scale; monitored examples retained **55%** of warm-season rainfall on a shallow roof and cut roof-deck temperature by **2.9 °C** in Shanghai. Effects vary materially with rain size, prior wetness, depth, climate and irrigation. \[1\] \[3\]
- **MEASURED — real façade monitoring; REPORTED — in-situ observational study.** A monitored Madrid living wall reduced the temperature behind its south façade by up to **20 °C in summer**, but a six-month, 12-site study found **no significant outdoor PM or air-temperature difference** beside passive green walls. Thermal shielding is supported; passive-wall claims of ambient-air cleaning/cooling are not. \[4\] \[5\]
- **REPORTED — operational/experimental rooftop farming.** Food can grow on roofs, but it is not a high-yield or maintenance-free use of all spare roof area: documented outdoor rooftop-farm yields span **4.4–6.1 kg/m²** in one experiment and **5.1–14.3 kg/m²/year** across reviewed operating farms; a Barcelona rooftop greenhouse reported **19.6 kg tomato/m²/year**. \[11\] \[12\]
- **REPORTED — field ecology and systematic review.** Flower-rich, structurally varied roofs can be habitat patches: 20 Antwerp roofs held **40 wild-bee species**. They did not establish hoverfly habitat well, did not prove nesting, and cannot substitute for ground habitat or a coastal ecological network. \[8\] \[9\]
- **MEASURED — meta-analysis; REPORTED — matched observational hospital study and office field studies.** Greenery may improve experience and some health-related outcomes, but productivity and clinical claims are not universally established. Indoor-plant meta-analysis found a small pooled diastolic-BP reduction (**−2.526 mmHg**, only three studies); a famous hospital tree-view result was non-random; one call-centre field experiment found no significant productivity difference. \[17\] \[18\] \[19\]
- **MEASURED — controlled experiments; UNKNOWN — Dongjiakou-specific exposure.** Salt, wind, thin substrate and irrigation quality are design-limiting. A salt/irrigation experiment used **6–18 dS/m** water and showed combined drought/salt stress changes evapotranspiration; it does **not** validate seawater, desalination brine, or any plant palette at Dongjiakou. \[21\]
- **REPORTED — official policy.** Shandong requires sponge-city controls to be integrated through project approval and delivery, but describes green roofs for eligible new buildings as something to **promote**, not a universal mandate. The green-building roof/vertical-greenery credit applies to **civil buildings**, not industrial plants. No enforceable Qingdao/Dongjiakou roof-greening quota or site-specific cash incentive was located. \[27\] \[28\] \[29\]
- **MEASURED — small field experiment.** PV and vegetation can coexist: two-month tests found green-roof PV output **0.7–1.2%** above black/white roof comparators and panels **1.5–3 °C** cooler. This is a modest, site-specific gain, not evidence that green roofs should displace viable solar capacity. \[26\]
> **Evidence labels used throughout:** **MEASURED** means an instrumented or controlled study with an observed outcome; **REPORTED** means an observational, operational, policy, review, or pilot finding; **MODELED** means an estimate produced by a model rather than observed outcome; **UNKNOWN** means this register found no adequate number or evidence for Dongjiakou. “Marketing” is identified as **UNKNOWN** where no outcome evidence supports it.
## Direct answer
**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** **MEASURED** evidence supports targeted green roofs, flower-rich habitat roofs, façades and roof farming as ways to reduce local roof heat, retain/delay some rain, create usable greenery and produce a limited amount of food. **REPORTED** evidence supports modest wellbeing and selected biodiversity gains. **UNKNOWN** evidence prevents any claim that the full coastal industrial node will be net-positive on water, food safety, air quality, biodiversity, health, cost or quality of life without site measurement and operations data. The strongest near-term case is **non-solar roof areas that pass structural, wind, salt, drainage and water-quality review**, not blanket greening or food production over industrial emission sources.

### 1.3 Air quality: separate leaf capture, roof concentration and city claims
**REPORTED — real but weakly generalisable pre/post observation.** A Singapore field study summarized by Yang and colleagues measured **6% lower particle** and **37% lower SO₂** concentrations above a 4,000-m² roof after installation. Its air volume of influence was not stated, it was not a randomised/control study, and it cannot establish city-scale removal. \[7\]
**MODELED — commonly repeated headline, not a measurement.** Yang et al. estimated **85 kg/ha/year** pollutant removal for Chicago green roofs using a dry-deposition model. The **1,675 kg/year** total for 19.8 ha and the extrapolated city-wide benefits are model outputs, not measured ambient health improvements. \[7\]
**MEASURED — matched real-roof monitoring with mixed outcomes.** An extensive Sydney biosolar roof and a proximal near-identical conventional roof were monitored across three seasons. Ozone was lower at the green roof, suspended PM had **no significant average difference**, and NO₂ was at times higher near the green roof, attributed largely to local construction. The reported **3.82 g O₃/m²/year**, **1.29 g NO₂/m²/year** and **0.29 g PM₂.₅/m²/year** removal rates are calculated from field concentrations, so they are **MODELED** removal estimates rather than captured-mass measurements. \[6\]
**MEASURED — consequential null result for passive walls.** Across 12 green-wall and nearby reference-wall locations over six months, Paull et al. found **no significant difference** in ambient PM or temperature. Noise was lower at green-wall locations, but the paper does not provide a portable dB effect size in its abstract. \[5\] This directly weakens the popular claim that a passive decorative wall reliably cleans or cools outdoor industrial air.
### 1.4 Flowers, trees and biodiversity
**MEASURED — field biodiversity use, not a net biodiversity claim.** Twenty green roofs in Antwerp contained **40 wild-bee species**. Only **11 hoverfly individuals from eight species** were captured. Roof area, height, age, surrounding green cover and Sedum-only versus mixed herb/grass cover did not significantly explain bee richness or abundance in that sample. The investigators state that their sampling cannot show whether bees nested on roofs or merely foraged there. \[8\]
**REPORTED — systematic-review conclusion.** The empirical literature indicates that green roofs can benefit urban biodiversity “to some extent,” but cannot replace former natural habitats or complex ground-level greenery. The reviewed studies concentrated on plants and arthropods and were overwhelmingly in the United States and United Kingdom. \[9\]
**UNKNOWN — trees on roofs and ecological connection.** No controlled Dongjiakou study was found that establishes survival, bird use, pollinator outcomes or movement connectivity for rooftop trees, flowering walls or roofs next to steel, chemical and LNG operations. A claim that individual roofs create a functional wildlife corridor is therefore **UNKNOWN**, not supported by the Antwerp bee count or the review.
**REPORTED — coastal-native planting is plausible, not prevalidated locally.** In Japan, researchers planted 13 rocky-seacoast species in 15-cm substrates over a 5-cm reservoir and followed cover for more than two years. Species outcomes ranged from vigorous growth to disappearance; cuttings worked better than seed for several species, and the installation used irrigation. \[20\] This supports trialling locally appropriate coastal plants; it does not identify a Qingdao palette or prove salt-spray survival.

### 2.2 Food safety in an industrial setting
**MEASURED — reassuring but non-transferable rooftop crop study.** Hydroponic lettuce grown in Barcelona near high-density roads had Pb of **0.0060–0.0244 mg/kg** — **6–24%** of the EU limit — while Ni, Cd and As were below stated detection levels. The study’s air metals were below 50% of the relevant lower assessment threshold. \[15\] It provides evidence that a **soilless system with controlled inputs** can produce compliant lettuce in that city; it does not certify food grown next to Dongjiakou’s industrial emissions.
**MEASURED — industrial-district rooftop farm, with a different exposure profile.** At Brooklyn Grange in New York’s former Navy Yard industrial district, farm-wide average metal concentrations did not exceed guideline levels; Pb and Mn drainage were **6%** and **14%** of atmospheric bulk deposition, respectively. Pb and Cr were relatively higher during fallow periods when soil was bare. \[16\] The authors recommend covered soil/mulch and disposal rather than composting of used mulch and unmarketable plant material to reduce deposition recycling. \[16\]
**UNKNOWN — food safety decision rule for Dongjiakou.** No located study measures edible-crop metals, PAHs, salt deposition, industrial dust or microbiological quality at Dongjiakou. Food agriculture should therefore be classified **UNKNOWN** until a seasonal baseline and pilot measure air/deposition, substrate, irrigation water, edible portions and drainage against the applicable standards. The Chinese farm-irrigation standard GB 5084-2021 governs irrigation-water quality, monitoring and supervision; it is not a substitution for crop and deposition testing. \[30\]
## 3. Views, indoor plants, work and hospital outcomes
### 3.1 Green views and hospital outcomes
**REPORTED — matched retrospective observational study, not a clinical trial.** Ulrich matched 23 pairs of cholecystectomy patients in similar rooms, allocated by room vacancy, with tree versus brick-wall views. The tree-view group had mean stays of **7.96 versus 8.70 days** and different analgesic patterns on postoperative days 2–5. \[17\] The result is important and quantified, but it is from one hospital, a non-random historical record review, a particular surgery and a monotonous-wall comparator. It is not medical advice and should not be generalised to all patients, views or healthcare settings.
**REPORTED — broad but heterogeneous literature.** A 2024 scoping review found 43 inpatient nature-exposure studies, including 13 RCTs, 12 retrospective cohorts and multiple other designs. It did not provide a pooled causal effect size because exposures, outcomes and designs were diverse. \[18\] **UNKNOWN — evidence contested by heterogeneity:** a universal clinical effect size for views cannot be stated from this literature.
### 3.2 Indoor plants, workplace experience and productivity
**MEASURED — systematic review with small evidence base.** Meta-analysis of indoor-plant studies reported a pooled diastolic blood-pressure difference of **−2.526 mmHg** (95% CI −4.142 to −0.909) and an academic-achievement effect estimate of **0.534** (95% CI 0.167–0.901). The authors included only 16 records in meta-analyses and only two or three studies per outcome; they rated the pertinent primary studies moderate quality. \[19\] The result supports a small potential physiological/cognitive benefit, not a clinical treatment claim.
**REPORTED — field experiments with a meaningful contradiction.** Three green-versus-lean office experiments reported better perceived air quality, concentration and satisfaction in planted offices. A public summary reports **15%** higher productivity, but its 81-person call-centre experiment found the productivity measure **not significantly different**; a 33-person task experiment found faster completion without more errors. \[20\] Thus “plants raise employee productivity by 15%” is **UNKNOWN as a general workplace claim**, not an established rate.
**REPORTED — larger pragmatic office field study.** A Before–After–Control–Impact study in nine Dutch organisations found fewer dry-air complaints, higher perceived privacy, attractiveness and workspace satisfaction, and fewer health-related complaints after plants. It found **no direct plant effect on 13 other outcomes** in its causal model, including downstream outcomes such as mental health, absenteeism and job satisfaction. \[21\] This is stronger than decoration marketing for immediate experience, but weak support for productivity, absenteeism or clinical-health guarantees.
**UNKNOWN — specific effects for AI and non-human inhabitants.** No applicable outcome literature was located on “quality of life” for AI systems from flora or views. This report cannot transfer human wellbeing results to AI. Effects on non-human residents must be evaluated through welfare, habitat and mortality indicators rather than aesthetic analogy.
## 4. Coastal industrial constraints: salt, wind, structure, water and maintenance
### 4.1 Salt, wind and substrate
**MEASURED — controlled salt/irrigation experiment.** In a glasshouse test of *Sedum kamtschaticum* in a 10-cm green-roof bed, irrigation salinity of **6–18 dS/m** plus dry intervals reduced evapotranspiration; the CAM plant improved water-use efficiency under stress. \[22\] The study also explains that shallow roof beds can accumulate salt because evapotranspiration concentrates salts, and leaching/frequent irrigation create a water-management trade-off. \[22\]
**REPORTED — coastal analogue, not a Dongjiakou test.** Coastal plants experience rapid drainage, wind-driven evaporation and salt spray. The Japanese coastal-roof trial used 15-cm substrate, a reservoir and irrigation, and still found divergent survival/growth across the 13 planted species. \[20\] **UNKNOWN:** salt deposition, gust speed, windborne industrial particulates, rainfall chemistry, freeze–thaw exposure and plant survival at each Dongjiakou roof and wall.
**UNKNOWN — untreated seawater or desalination brine as irrigation.** The located saline study does not validate either water source, and no Dongjiakou crop/ornamental trial was found. The result should not be cited as permission to irrigate with seawater or brine. Any non-potable water proposal requires chemistry measurement, plant-specific salinity/boron assessment, corrosion review, drainage/leachate management and compliance screening.
### 4.2 Structural load and plant type
**REPORTED — Chinese review, useful only as an early screening range.** A 2007 China/Germany–Beijing review describes extensive systems as **60–200 kg/m²**, semi-intensive as **120–250 kg/m²**, and intensive systems as **150–1,000 kg/m²**; its associated typical total-depth ranges are 6–20 cm, 12–25 cm and 15–100 cm. \[25\] These are literature/review ranges, not load allowances, current codes or safe design values for an industrial roof.
**UNKNOWN — every Dongjiakou roof’s allowable added dead, saturated, wind-uplift and maintenance load.** No structural drawings, roof condition, drainage capacity, waterproofing/root barrier details, equipment loads, snow/rain load combinations or fire/access constraints were supplied. Trees, deep beds, water-storage crates, greenhouse frames and solar ballast must therefore be treated as **UNKNOWN feasibility** until a licensed structural and roof-envelope assessment identifies each roof’s capacity.
**REPORTED — maintenance intensity rises with productive/deeper systems.** The Chinese review characterises extensive roofs as low-maintenance/low-irrigation and intensive roofs as requiring frequent maintenance and irrigation. \[25\] **MEASURED:** field agriculture and green-wall studies confirm that even managed systems face disease, irrigation non-uniformity and nutrient/leachate management. \[11\] \[24\]
### 4.3 Maintenance and water burden
**MEASURED — living-wall irrigation failure mode.** A 109-day Madrid living-wall study derived landscape coefficients from **0.32 in winter to 0.6 in summer**. Its drip/percolation system left an upper **12%** of wall area water-deficient and a lower **88%** wetter and prone to root asphyxia. \[24\] This is concrete evidence that a wall requires metered irrigation, drainage, inspection and seasonal adjustment; automated irrigation is not zero-maintenance.
**MEASURED — water use is not optional for high-performance planted roofs.** The monitored irrigated roof described above used up to **19 mm/week** in summer in one planting zone. \[23\] This is a site-specific measured burden, not an estimate for Dongjiakou. **UNKNOWN:** annual m³, source-water cost, treatment energy and chemical/fertiliser load for the proposed node.
**UNKNOWN — net water, energy and maintenance balance.** No design-level inventory exists for planted area, crop/wall system, irrigation source, roof area, rainfall, replacement plants, labour, pump energy or drainage quality. Claims that greenery “saves water” or is “maintenance-free” are **UNKNOWN (marketing when unquantified)**.

## 6. Solar versus greenery: conflict and combination
**MEASURED — combined use can work.** A two-month Portland experiment compared identical PV panels over green, black and white roof surfaces at 18-cm and 24-cm mounting heights. At 18 cm, the green-roof panel’s mean output was **1.2%** above black and **0.8%** above white; at 24 cm the differences were **1.0%** and **0.7%**. Green-roof panels were **1.5–3 °C** cooler, and reported differences were statistically significant. \[26\]
**MEASURED — the trade-off is explicit.** The same study warns that panels placed too close to vegetation risk plant overgrowth and panel shading, while benefits diminish with increasing panel–roof distance. \[26\] That is a real design conflict: a roof cannot simultaneously maximise crop/tree canopy, solar access, maintenance access and low structural load in the same square metre.
**UNKNOWN — optimal allocation at Dongjiakou.** No roof inventory, PV yield model, electrical interconnection constraint, structural assessment, shade study or biodiversity/water objective weighting was provided. No evidence supports a universal “solar first” or “green first” rule. A defensible allocation requires comparing: (1) PV-only, (2) extensive flowering roof, (3) biosolar strip layout, (4) intensive food roof, and (5) no intervention, using the same roof-specific structural, energy, water, habitat and maintenance boundaries.

## Evidence-grade conclusion
**Evidence quality: MIXED EVIDENCE.** **STRONG** for instrumented roof runoff/thermal effects and façade-surface shielding, because multiple real-installation studies measure them. **MODERATE** for roof agriculture and pollinator habitat use, because real sites exist but yield/ecology are system- and location-specific. **WEAK to CONTESTED** for passive outdoor-air cleaning, general workplace productivity, broad clinical outcomes and net-positive lifecycle claims. **UNKNOWN** for Dongjiakou coastal durability, potable/non-potable water balance, food safety, structural suitability and operating economics.
