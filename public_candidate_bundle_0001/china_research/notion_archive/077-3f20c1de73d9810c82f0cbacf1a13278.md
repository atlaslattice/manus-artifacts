## Executive summary
- **\[MEASURED\]** A laboratory/batch study that simulated LNG-cold integration reported product-ice TDS of about **300 mg/L** after optimizing a freshwater wash at **50 wt% of raw ice**. This is a strong proof that washed ice *can* have low TDS, but it is not a commercial-scale or full potable-water compliance result. \[1\]
- **\[MEASURED\]** Ice quality is highly process-dependent: a 150 L/h prototype reported only about **50% one-cycle salt removal** and explicitly said that one cycle was not enough for drinking water, while a separate simplified process targeted **irrigation water (\<2,000 mg/L TDS)** using 45–50% wash water. Thus, a generic “90–99% salt rejection” claim is not verified for an industrial standard-ice-washing system. \[4\] \[7\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** The WHO does **not** set a health-based TDS guideline. It says TDS below 1,000 mg/L is usually acceptable to consumers. China’s GB 5749-2022 sets a 1,000 mg/L TDS limit, but also requires compliant microbiology, chemicals, sensory parameters, and disinfection. A TDS result alone cannot establish potable-water compliance. \[2\] \[3\]
- **\[MEASURED\]** The best directly relevant wash requirement found is 50 wt% of raw ice in Chang et al.; a review synthesizing several studies gives **40–50 wt%**. This is a very large product-water inventory requirement, not a measured permanent loss fraction. The irreversible water loss after wash-water recycle is **UNKNOWN**. \[1\] \[9\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** Historical vacuum-freezing/vapour-compression pilot experience was not low-electricity in practice: at least **12 kWh(e)/m³** was reported, versus a theoretical 3 kWh(e)/m³ total. The historical source assigns roughly 1 kWh(e)/m³ in theory to cooling, venting, pumping, and controls combined; it does not provide a transferable modern wash/pump-only number. \[10\]
- **\[MODELED\]** The latent heat alone to freeze 1 m³ of water is about **91.7 kWh(th)/m³** (330 kJ/kg), before sensible cooling, heat-exchanger losses, ice melting, or eutectic concentration. LNG cold could supply part of this *thermal duty*, but it does not make pumps, slurry/ice separation, wash circulation, controls, or any refrigeration temperature lift electrically free. \[9\]
- **\[MODELED\]** For the binary NaCl–water approximation, the eutectic is **23.3 wt% at −21.1 °C**. If the cold stream entering the freeze step is actually about −20 °C, it cannot provide a finite-temperature driving force to reach that eutectic without colder utility or refrigeration. The proposed “−5 °C out” therefore does not demonstrate eutectic operation. Actual multicomponent RO-brine phase behavior is **UNKNOWN** until assayed and modelled/measured. \[9\]
- **\[REPORTED\]** No continuously operating commercial freeze-desalination plant was identified. Named systems found are laboratory/prototype/pilot units—HybridICE™, the Lin–Huang–Gu 150 L/h prototype, and a 3 L/850 mL-per-cycle system described as a pilot continuous unit—not commercial plants. Two reviews state that freeze desalination has not reached commercial scale. \[5\] \[7\] \[8\] \[9\] \[11\]
## Bottom-line answer
**\[MEASURED\]** Freeze desalination can produce low-TDS ice water under controlled experimental conditions. The clearest standard-wash result found is approximately 300 mg/L TDS after a 50 wt% raw-ice wash in a batch experiment. \[1\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** This does **not** establish that ice produced from the proposed Dongjiakou approximately 6.6 wt% RO brine will consistently meet GB 5749-2022, WHO acceptability, or any irrigation standard. No Dongjiakou-brine ionic assay, contaminants analysis, microbiology, product-water test, wash-water-recycle design, or continuous industrial demonstration was supplied or located. The 14 million m³/year product claim therefore cannot be validated from the freeze-desalination evidence alone.
**\[REPORTED\]** The literature supports a more limited conclusion: high salt removal is achievable in selected bench/pilot configurations, but standard freshwater washing consumes roughly 40–50% of raw-ice mass, reported electricity figures have incompatible boundaries, and commercial continuous operation remains unproven. \[1\] \[5\] \[9\] \[10\] \[11\]
## 1. What “salt rejection” results actually show
### 1.1 The 90–99% range is real in individual experiments, but it is not a universal industrial performance guarantee
**\[MEASURED\]** Chang, Zuo, Lu and Chung tested artificial and real seawater in a batch indirect-contact freeze-desalination experiment intended to inform LNG-cold use. Their optimized condition used wash water equal to about **50 wt% of raw ice**. The reported thawed product-ice TDS was approximately **300 ppm**. They also reported more than 50% raw-ice recovery from feed within one hour and an overall ice recovery above 25% after accounting for wash-water consumption. This is a **laboratory/batch** result, not a continuously operated plant result. \[1\]
**\[MEASURED\]** The HybridICE™ pilot study treated synthetic NaCl brines from 2.3 to 10 wt% in batch and continuous modes. It reported **80–96% ice purity**; a related HybridICE pilot paper reported **96% salt removal based on average chloride concentration in ice**. These are pilot measurements, but they are neither a full multi-ion seawater/RO-brine analysis nor a potable-water certification. The technology is explicitly trademarked; no independent, long-duration commercial validation was identified, so the result should not be treated as an independently proven plant guarantee. \[5\] \[6\]
**\[MEASURED\]** The Lin, Huang and Gu prototype was designed at 150 L/h and tested with liquid nitrogen as the cold source, rather than an operating LNG terminal. Its one-cycle salt-removal rate was only about **50%**, which the authors said was insufficient for drinking water. It is direct evidence against using a single high salt-rejection figure without specifying the crystallizer, wash/separation method, number of stages, and feed. \[7\]
**\[MEASURED\]** Shin et al. used synthetic 35 g/L NaCl feed in a surface-scraped freeze crystallizer. Their optimized 45–50% washing-water conditions were framed as producing **irrigation water below 2,000 mg/L TDS**, not potable water. This is an experimental result and shows that the same broad technology family can deliver a lower grade than the Chang study. \[4\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The review by Najim reports 93% removal with washing, 95% with sweating, and 98% with centrifuging from a cited study of **cyclopentane hydrate** desalination. Those figures should **not** be used as standard ice-freeze-desalination washing performance: hydrate crystals, a hydrocarbon former, and the stated separation steps are materially different from ice crystallization and washing. \[9\]

## 2. Product-water grade: TDS is necessary but not sufficient
### 2.1 WHO and Chinese drinking-water benchmarks
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO’s TDS background document says that water with TDS below **1,000 mg/L** is usually acceptable to consumers, but it explicitly proposes **no health-based guideline value** for TDS. Its discussion is principally palatability and scaling, and it says individual constituents must be identified where TDS is high. The often repeated “WHO 500 mg/L potable standard” is therefore not supported by this WHO source. \[2\]
**\[REPORTED\]** GB 5749-2022 sets TDS at **1,000 mg/L**. It also sets chloride at 250 mg/L, sodium at 200 mg/L, microbial limits including non-detectable total coliforms and *E. coli*, limits for toxicological indicators, sensory requirements, and a disinfection requirement. The English text used here is an unofficial USDA Foreign Agricultural Service translation of the Chinese national standard; the underlying standard entered into force on 1 April 2023. \[3\]
**\[MEASURED\]** The approximately 300 mg/L TDS product reported by Chang et al. is numerically below both the WHO “usually acceptable” TDS level and GB 5749-2022’s TDS limit. \[1\] \[2\] \[3\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** It cannot be called GB 5749-compliant drinking water. The accessible Chang record does not provide the full GB 5749 analyte suite, microbiology, disinfectant residual, pH, chloride, sodium, boron, metals, or a distribution-system compliance test. Its statement that 300 ppm met a “WHO potable water salinity standard of 500 ppm” must be read as the authors’ interpretation, not as confirmation of current WHO guidance or Chinese regulatory compliance. \[1\] \[2\] \[3\]
### 2.2 Irrigation is a lower, not automatically demonstrated, endpoint
**\[MEASURED\]** Shin et al. explicitly used a \<2,000 mg/L TDS target for irrigation water in their laboratory process. This supports only the authors’ experimental target and does not certify any Chinese irrigation-water standard. \[4\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No source was found that maps the reported freeze-desalination products to a specific crop, soil, irrigation method, sodium-adsorption ratio, boron limit, or applicable Chinese irrigation category. A TDS-only result cannot establish irrigation suitability either.
### 2.3 Implication for the proposed RO-brine case
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No direct source was found measuring washed ice quality from a **6.6 wt% Dongjiakou SWRO brine**. RO brine is not necessarily a binary NaCl solution; calcium, magnesium, sulfate, potassium, boron, antiscalant residues, trace metals, organics, and upstream contamination can control both phase behavior and product-water quality. A feed assay and product-suite testing are required before declaring potable or irrigation grade.
## 3. Wash-water requirement and actual water loss
**\[MEASURED\]** Chang et al. found an optimum fresh wash-water amount of about **50 wt% of raw ice**. In their batch calculation, more than half of feed became raw ice within one hour, while the reported overall ice recovery was higher than 25% after allowing for wash-water consumption. \[1\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Najim’s review states that freshwater needed to obtain potable water by washing after suspension freeze concentration ranges from **40% to 50 wt% of the ice obtained**, citing Chang, Shin, and Sahu. The review concludes that this large wash requirement is a material economic limitation. \[9\]
**\[MEASURED\]** Shin et al. independently identified 45–50% washing-water percentages as their optimum range for an irrigation-water result, consistent with the large wash inventory in Chang et al. \[4\]
**\[MEASURED\]** A different HybridICE™ filter was designed to separate ice slurry by buoyancy and reported that it required **no water washing**. It was evaluated at 25 L/min for mine-wastewater treatment. This is a different separation architecture; it does not invalidate the 40–50% requirement for the standard wash processes above. \[12\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The permanent product-water loss fraction during washing is not reported in a form that can be transferred to the proposed plant. “50 wt% wash water” is a wash-water **circulation/consumption requirement**, not proof that 50% of produced ice is irreversibly lost. Whether the wash is once-through, recycled counter-currently, recovered as product after treatment, or discarded with brine determines net loss. No project mass balance or source quantifying net loss as a percentage of product ice was found.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The corresponding annual wash-water flow and net water yield for the claimed roughly 14 million m³/year ice product cannot be calculated honestly without a defined raw-ice production rate, wash configuration, recycle recovery, product-water specification, and purge rate.

A single “kWh/m³ for freeze desalination” value is therefore not transferable to the Dongjiakou concept. \[9\] \[10\] \[11\]

### 4.2 What LNG cold changes—and does not change
**\[MEASURED\]** Lin et al. built a 150 L/h prototype and reported more than 2 kg fresh water per kg LNG-equivalent cold-energy efficiency, but the experiments used **liquid nitrogen** as cold source. The same paper says one-cycle salt removal was only about 50%. It is laboratory/prototype evidence for a heat-transfer concept, not a measurement at an LNG receiving terminal. \[7\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The review literature identifies LNG cold as an opportunity to reduce the refrigeration portion of freeze desalination. It does not provide a measured, commercial LNG-terminal auxiliary-electricity balance for washing, pumping, separation, and ice handling. \[9\]
**\[MODELED\]** Treating refrigeration duty as “free” because LNG must be regasified is incomplete. The most that can be set to zero in a project energy balance is an **assumed marginal cost of supplied cold duty**; electrical equipment and any refrigerator needed to reach a lower temperature still consume power. The historical VFVC gap between roughly 3 theoretical and at least 12 actual kWh(e)/m³ is a warning that operational parasitics and inefficiencies matter. \[10\]
### 4.3 Eutectic temperature is a cascade compatibility constraint
**\[REPORTED\]** For binary NaCl–water, the freeze-concentration path reaches the eutectic at **23.3 wt% NaCl and −21.1 °C**. At eutectic conditions, ice and salt crystallize simultaneously; the review describes ice floating and salt crystals settling, which facilitates physical separation. \[9\]
**\[MODELED\]** If the freeze-stage cold utility truly enters at approximately −20 °C, it is warmer than the reported −21.1 °C binary eutectic. It cannot cool a stream to the eutectic with a positive heat-exchanger temperature driving force. A colder source, an added refrigeration stage, or a less concentrated stopping point would be required. This is a thermodynamic inference from the reported phase diagram; it must be checked against the actual stream temperatures and a multicomponent brine model. \[9\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The actual eutectic temperature and sequence of salt precipitation for the proposed RO brine are unknown. The 23.3 wt% number applies to the **binary NaCl–water approximation**, not automatically to a real SWRO concentrate.

## 6. Residual stream at nominal eutectic concentration
**\[REPORTED\]** In the binary NaCl–water representation, the liquid reaches 23.3 wt% at −21.1 °C. Further eutectic freeze crystallization produces both ice and salt crystals rather than a simple higher-salinity liquid brine. \[9\]
**\[MEASURED\]** A 2025 laboratory EFC study on an RO brine detected ice plus salt hydrates including mirabilite (Na₂SO₄·10H₂O) and hydrohalite (NaCl·10H₂O). It reported approximately 88% water recovery and yields of 16.7 g/L mirabilite and 79.4 g/L hydrohalite in that study. This demonstrates that multicomponent brine does not behave as pure NaCl; it is laboratory evidence, not a saleable-salt specification. \[13\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A U.S. Bureau of Reclamation bench study concentrated an electrodialysis brine to 23% salinity, recovered about three quarters of its NaCl as \>99.5% crystals after treatment, and left a bromine-rich bittern for subsequent treatment. The study also warns that the economics of recovering low-concentration constituents can be poor. It is a different ED/evaporation process, but it is relevant evidence that a 23 wt% salt stream requires staged separation and leaves a residual liquor. \[14\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The proposed residual stream’s mass, phase split, temperature, salt-hydrate composition, contaminants, waste classification, discharge permit route, marine dilution analysis, salt-product purity, and marketability are unknown. It cannot responsibly be described as simply “23.3 wt% brine for disposal” or as a saleable NaCl product.
**\[REPORTED\]** The technically credible handling sequence is therefore: separate ice from salt crystals/liquor; wash and analyse the solids; identify any mother-liquor/bittern purge; then select permitted reuse, further resource recovery, controlled crystallization, or a licensed discharge/disposal route. EFC literature supports recovery of solids and water as a possibility, not as an automatic zero-liquid-discharge outcome. \[9\] \[13\] \[14\]
## 7. Evidence limits and decision implication
**\[MEASURED\]** The literature demonstrates both a low-TDS washed-ice outcome (about 300 mg/L) and lower-quality outcomes (about 50% one-cycle removal; irrigation target \<2,000 mg/L). The result is controlled by feed composition, freezing rate, ice/brine separation, wash design, and number of cycles. \[1\] \[4\] \[7\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Review evidence does not support presenting freeze desalination with standard water washing as a commercially established, continuously operated desalination block. \[9\] \[11\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** For the Dongjiakou proposal, product grade, wash loss, auxiliary electricity, actual cold availability after air separation, eutectic attainability, residual disposal, and annual production are all unverified. The evidence supports a **pilot test proposition**, not a bankable 14 million m³/year potable-water or low-auxiliary-power claim.
