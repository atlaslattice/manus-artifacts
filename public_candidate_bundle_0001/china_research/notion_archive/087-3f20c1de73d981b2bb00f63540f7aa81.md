## Executive summary
- **MEASURED — SUPPORTIVE BUT LIMITED:** In an individual-patient meta-analysis of 39 adequately concealed RCTs (20,827 people), acupuncture’s chronic-pain benefit was about **0.2 SD versus sham** and about **0.5 SD versus no acupuncture**; effects were smaller against a more active/penetrating sham. This supports a small specific component for some chronic-pain conditions, not a large general health effect. \[S1\]
- **MEASURED — CONTESTED:** A three-arm pain review found true acupuncture versus sham differed by **4 mm on a 100-mm pain scale** (SMD −0.17), while sham versus no acupuncture differed by SMD −0.42; its authors judged the specific difference unlikely to be clinically relevant and potentially indistinguishable from bias. The amount attributable to “placebo” cannot be validly expressed as a percentage because sham acupuncture is not inert. \[S2\]
- **MEASURED — SUPPORTIVE:** Tai chi, **not qigong**, has the clearest functional evidence: 24 RCTs found fewer older adult fallers (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.71–0.82), and a knee-osteoarthritis meta-analysis reported improvements in pain, function and balance with low-to-moderate certainty. \[S9\] \[S10\]
- **MEASURED — TENTATIVE:** Qigong has smaller, more heterogeneous literatures. For sleep, 13 RCTs (1,147 participants) produced an outlier-sensitive effect (Hedges’ g −0.423 after removal; 95% CI −0.603 to −0.243); half used no active control. A stress review found no significant advantage over active controls and generally low trial quality. \[S6\] \[S7\]
- **MEASURED — SPECIFIC INDICATIONS, NOT A UNIVERSAL CLAIM:** Moderate-quality Cochrane evidence supports a small benefit over sham for episodic migraine prevention (SMD −0.18); by contrast, Cochrane’s chronic non-specific low-back-pain review found the sham-controlled pain difference (−9.22/100) below its 15-point clinical-importance threshold. \[S4\] \[S3\]
- **MEASURED/REPORTED — PRODUCTIVITY NOT ESTABLISHED:** Controlled evidence does not establish that acupuncture, qigong or tai chi increases workplace productivity, lowers absence, or reduces presenteeism. The small workplace tai chi literature includes a 6-versus-5 nurse pilot reporting a 3% productivity rise but non-significant between-group stress differences; it cannot substantiate an operational productivity claim. \[S12\]
- **MEASURED — RISK IS REAL, USUALLY MINOR BUT NOT ZERO:** Prospective acupuncture studies pooled at least one adverse event in 9.31% of people receiving a course; serious events were estimated at 1.01 per 10,000 patients and 7.98 per million treatments, with substantial heterogeneity. Pneumothorax, vasovagal events/falls, infection and organ injury are documented, the last two mainly in case reports rather than those prospective cohorts. \[S5\]
- **REPORTED — POLICY/FINANCING IS NOT EFFICACY VALIDATION:** China’s policy promotes TCM and traditional exercise; national insurance policy directs local inclusion of qualifying TCM services. Shandong reports acupuncture and therapeutic massage are within fund payment scope, while **health-maintenance/wellness services are explicitly outside basic medical insurance**. Coverage is conditional on local catalogues, indications, provider and payment rules. \[S16\] \[S17\] \[S18\]
## Claim labels and evidence discipline
**MEASURED** denotes an outcome measured in a controlled trial or a systematic review/meta-analysis of such trials. **REPORTED** denotes policy, administrative, observational, safety-surveillance or author-reported information. **MODELED** would denote a forecast or derived economic estimate; none is used here. **UNKNOWN** denotes an evidence gap; it is not an estimate.
**MEASURED:** “Qigong” and “tai chi” are kept separate throughout. Reviews often combine them under traditional Chinese exercise or meditative movement; that aggregation cannot establish that their effects, dose, mechanisms or evidence certainty are interchangeable. This report therefore uses only modality-specific results for the main verdict. \[S6\] \[S7\] \[S9\] \[S11\]
**REPORTED:** This is an evidence review, not medical advice, a clinical protocol, or a finding that any intervention is appropriate for a particular person.
## 1. Acupuncture
### 1.1 What is supported for defined clinical outcomes
**MEASURED — chronic pain:** The Acupuncture Trialists’ Collaboration individual-patient-data meta-analysis included 39 RCTs and 20,827 participants with nonspecific musculoskeletal pain, osteoarthritis, chronic headache or shoulder pain. All trials had unambiguously adequate allocation concealment. Relative to sham, fixed-effect standardized benefits were 0.30 for musculoskeletal pain, 0.24 for osteoarthritis, 0.16 for chronic headache and 0.57 for shoulder pain; relative to no acupuncture they were 0.54, 0.63 and 0.44 respectively (no no-acupuncture shoulder meta-analysis). Heterogeneity was present in several comparisons. \[S1\]
**MEASURED — chronic pain:** The same review’s overall characterization was effects near **0.2 SD against sham** and near **0.5 SD against no acupuncture**, with an estimated approximately 15% reduction in treatment effect at one year. This is controlled evidence of a modest average difference, not evidence that acupuncture cures chronic pain or improves every pain condition. \[S1\]
**MEASURED — episodic migraine prevention:** Cochrane’s 2016 review of 22 trials (4,985 participants) judged overall evidence moderate. Against sham, true acupuncture reduced migraine frequency by SMD −0.18 (95% CI −0.28 to −0.08) after treatment and −0.19 (−0.30 to −0.09) at follow-up; 50% versus 41% achieved at least a 50% frequency reduction after treatment (RR 1.23, 1.11–1.36). Against usual/no prophylactic treatment, 41% versus 17% achieved that response (RR 2.40, 2.08–2.76; NNTB 4). The much smaller sham contrast is central to interpretation. \[S4\]
**MEASURED — chronic nonspecific low-back pain, a material counterexample:** Cochrane’s review included 33 trials and 8,270 participants. Its sham-controlled short-term pain difference was MD −9.22/100 (95% CI −13.82 to −4.61; seven trials, 1,403 participants; low certainty), below its prespecified 15/100 or 30% clinical-importance threshold. Function (SMD −0.16, −0.38 to 0.06) and short-term quality of life did not show a clinically important sham-controlled improvement. Versus no treatment, pain and function benefits were larger; this pattern is compatible with important non-specific/contextual and activity effects, as well as any needling-specific effect. \[S3\]
**MEASURED — what remains disputed:** The chronic-pain individual-patient meta-analysis concludes pain reduction cannot be explained *solely* by placebo effects because of the sham contrast. The three-arm Madsen review concludes its much smaller specific difference may lack clinical relevance and cannot be clearly separated from bias. These are competing interpretations of overlapping methodological problems, not evidence that either “all placebo” or “large specific effect” is proven. \[S1\] \[S2\]
### 1.2 Sham, placebo and non-specific effects: what can and cannot be quantified
**MEASURED:** Sham acupuncture can include non-penetrating devices, superficial/penetrating needling, or needling at locations considered non-acupuncture points. In the Madsen three-arm review, practitioners were unblinded in every included trial and the authors ranked shams by possible physiological activity; no association between that ranking and the true-versus-sham contrast was detected. This does **not** establish that any sham was inert. \[S2\]
**MEASURED:** In Madsen et al.’s 13 eligible three-arm pain trials (3,025 people; one outlier excluded), true acupuncture versus sham was SMD −0.17 (95% CI −0.26 to −0.08), approximately 4 mm (2–6 mm) on a 100-mm visual-analogue pain scale. Sham versus no acupuncture was SMD −0.42 (−0.60 to −0.23), with substantial heterogeneity (I² 66%). \[S2\]
**MEASURED:** In the larger chronic-pain individual-patient data set, the 12 RCTs containing both sham and no-acupuncture arms found sham versus no-acupuncture effect sizes of 0.39 (95% CI 0.33–0.45, fixed effect) and 0.45 (0.29–0.61, random effect). The review also found smaller true-versus-sham effects when the sham used a penetrating needle. \[S1\]
**UNKNOWN — attribution:** No robust trial design can uniquely partition these contrasts into “needle-point-specific,” expectancy, attention, therapeutic ritual, spontaneous recovery, reporting bias and physiological stimulation caused by the sham. It is therefore **not valid** to say that a fixed percentage of the total effect is placebo or non-specific. The sham-versus-no-treatment contrast measures the consequence of receiving a credible treatment-like procedure, but is not a pure placebo estimate; the true-versus-sham contrast is the best available estimate of the incremental package difference, but may be attenuated by an active sham and affected by imperfect blinding. \[S1\] \[S2\]
**REPORTED — practical evidence-register interpretation:** Claims that acupuncture’s effects are wholly explained by placebo are stronger than the chronic-pain and migraine sham-controlled data support. Claims that sham-controlled evidence demonstrates a large, point-specific physiological effect are also stronger than the observed small contrasts support. \[S1\] \[S2\] \[S4\]
### 1.3 Stress, sleep and work outcomes
**UNKNOWN — sleep and work outcomes:** A pooled, high-certainty effect size for acupuncture on objective workplace sleep, accident rates, absence or presenteeism was not identified in the searches documented below. This is insufficient to promote acupuncture as a productivity intervention for the Dongjiakou workforce.
**MEASURED — stress:** A randomized controlled **pilot** trial in people with increased stress exists, but pilot evidence is not a systematic demonstration of occupational productivity, and no economic or production outcome is established by it. \[S20\]
**UNKNOWN — workplace productivity:** No systematic review or sufficiently powered controlled trial was located that demonstrates acupuncture increases output, reduces industrial error, reduces sickness absence, or reduces presenteeism in industrial, port, steel, chemical-park, data-centre or food-processing work. This is a documented null result, not evidence of no possible effect.
### 1.4 Acupuncture risk
**MEASURED — prospective safety evidence:** Bäumler et al. synthesized 21 prospective studies (22 articles). At least one adverse event was estimated in 9.31% (95% CI 5.10–14.62; 11 studies) of patients receiving a series and 7.57% (1.43–17.95; five studies) of treatments; definitions vary and I² exceeded 80%. Estimated serious-event frequency was 1.01 per 10,000 patients (0.23–2.33; 11 studies) and 7.98 per one million treatments (1.39–20.00; 14 studies). \[S5\]
**MEASURED — documented harms:** Common minor events include bleeding, needle-site pain, local reactions, autonomic/vasovagal reactions, symptom aggravation and central-nervous-system symptoms. The prospective review identified pneumothorax and strong cardiovascular/vasovagal reactions leading to falls or trauma as the serious events most often seen, at roughly one to three per million treatments; traumatic organ injury, local/systemic infections, subarachnoid bleeding, infective endocarditis and cardiac tamponade were repeatedly reported in case reports but were not observed in the included prospective cohorts. \[S5\]
**REPORTED — limitations of the risk numbers:** These prospective studies commonly involved trained practitioners, and causality for more than half of serious events was judged unlikely or unclassifiable by independent acupuncture experts. The pooled rates should therefore neither be read as zero risk nor directly generalized to unregulated provision, all anatomical sites, all techniques or all patient groups. \[S5\]
## 2. Qigong — distinct, smaller and more heterogeneous evidence base
### 2.1 Stress, mood and wellbeing
**MEASURED — stress:** A 2024 systematic review included nine qigong RCTs (799 participants) and judged only one high quality. In two no-treatment-controlled RCTs, the review reported a significant perceived-stress estimate of −0.60 (95% CI −1.02 to −0.17). In three active-control studies, it found no significant difference (−2.10, −4.68 to 0.47). The authors concluded that specific conclusions were difficult because interventions, outcomes and trial quality varied. \[S6\]
**MEASURED — depression and anxiety:** A 2013 systematic review of 12 qigong studies found depression estimates favouring qigong against waiting-list/usual care (SMD −0.75, 95% CI −1.44 to −0.06; five studies; I² 89%) and walking/conventional exercise (SMD −0.52, −0.85 to −0.19; three studies). The CBT comparison was non-significant (SMD 0.56, −1.25 to 2.37; two studies; I² 92%), and only one of four anxiety studies reported a significant group difference. Allocation concealment was adequate in five trials, outcome-assessor blinding in four, and intention-to-treat analysis in four. These are tentative symptom findings, not a demonstration of treatment for depressive or anxiety disorders. \[S8\]
**MEASURED — interpretation:** Better outcomes against waiting list/no treatment than against active comparators are consistent with exercise, social contact, attention, expectation, relaxation time, reporting effects or a mixture of these mechanisms. The literature does not isolate a unique “qi” mechanism or establish it as necessary for the measured outcomes. \[S6\] \[S8\]
### 2.2 Sleep
**MEASURED — subjective sleep quality:** Ko et al. identified 13 RCTs (1,147 participants) in English or Chinese. The all-study sleep-quality effect was Hedges’ g −0.955 (95% CI −1.601 to −0.309) with extreme heterogeneity (I² 95.686%). After excluding one outlier, the effect was g −0.423 (−0.603 to −0.243; I² 39.137%). The review explicitly notes that half the studies lacked an active control, so non-specific effects may explain part of the difference. \[S7\]
**UNKNOWN — objective sleep and work safety:** No high-certainty pooled evidence was located for qigong’s effect on polysomnography/actigraphy, shift-work sleep disorder, fatigue-related error, industrial incident rates, or workforce productivity. The self-reported sleep signal is not enough to infer those outcomes.
### 2.3 Qigong in workplaces
**MEASURED — preliminary controlled evidence, not productivity evidence:** The stress review includes a hospital-staff RCT (50 randomized; 37 analyzed) in which qigong versus no intervention reduced a perceived-stress score; it did not establish productivity, absence, presenteeism, safety performance or cost outcomes. \[S6\]
**UNKNOWN — general workplace effect:** No systematic review of adequately powered qigong workplace trials was located that reports a reliable controlled effect size for productivity, turnover, absenteeism, presenteeism, injury, error or payback. Promotional claims of workplace productivity gains therefore outrun the identified controlled evidence.
### 2.4 Qigong safety
**REPORTED:** The 2024 stress review reported no adverse events in two studies and mild muscle soreness in six participants (24%) in one study. This small and selective evidence base cannot quantify qigong harm rates across older adults, people with disability, or industrial settings. \[S6\]
## 3. Tai chi — do not merge this evidence with qigong
### 3.1 Falls and balance
**MEASURED — falls:** A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis of 24 tai chi RCTs found fewer fallers: 1,041/2,766 in tai chi groups versus 1,321/2,703 controls; RR 0.76 (95% CI 0.71–0.82; I² 25%). It also reported fewer falls (MD −0.26, −0.39 to −0.13), faster Timed Up and Go (MD −0.69, −1.09 to −0.29) and higher Functional Reach (MD 2.69, 1.14–4.24). \[S9\]
**REPORTED — limitation:** In the same review, 22 trials were low risk for sequence generation and 17 for allocation concealment, but only seven were low risk for participant blinding, which is inherently difficult for an exercise intervention. Nineteen used blinded outcome assessment. This supports a substantially more developed tai chi falls/balance literature than qigong’s, while not removing all risk of bias. \[S9\]
### 3.2 Chronic disease: condition-specific evidence
**MEASURED — knee osteoarthritis:** A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (986 patients) rated main-outcome certainty low or moderate. It reported tai chi improvements in pain (SMD −0.69, −0.95 to −0.44), stiffness (−0.59, −0.91 to −0.27), physical function (−0.92, −1.16 to −0.69), dynamic balance (0.69, 0.38–0.99), SF-36 physical component (0.48, 0.28–0.68) and mental component (0.26, 0.06–0.45). These findings support tai chi as an exercise/rehabilitation option for this defined population, not as a substitute for diagnosis or treatment. \[S10\]
**MEASURED — COPD:** Cochrane included 12 RCTs (984 participants; 811 used in the tai chi-versus-control analysis) and graded outcomes very low to moderate. Compared with usual care, tai chi increased six-minute walk distance by 29.64 m (95% CI 10.52–48.77; 318 participants) and FEV₁ by 0.11 L (0.02–0.20; 258 participants), while dyspnoea and quality-of-life effects remained inconclusive. Tai chi added to breathing exercise or exercise did not show superiority over those interventions alone. \[S11\]
**REPORTED — scope boundary:** The above is evidence for specific physical conditions and activity outcomes. It does not demonstrate that tai chi prevents all chronic disease, replaces rehabilitation, or has the same effect in healthy working-age adults.
### 3.3 Stress, sleep and workplace productivity
**MEASURED — workplace review:** A systematic review of tai chi in healthcare workers found only six papers: three clinical trials, one observational study, one systematic review and one case report. In the key nurse pilot, six tai chi participants versus five no-intervention controls had a reported 3% productivity increase, but the relative reductions in work stress (−20% versus −8.5%, p=0.89) and general stress (−23% versus −17.5%, p=0.42) were not significant. \[S12\]
**REPORTED — workplace review:** A 12-person pre/post study in the same review reported a mean mental-wellbeing gain of 4.13 (p\<0.005), and a 15-person short program reported improved sleep. Neither is a sufficiently powered, blinded or independently replicated productivity evaluation. \[S12\]
**UNKNOWN — productivity:** No reliable controlled effect size was identified for tai chi on industrial productivity, sickness absence, presenteeism, work errors, incident rates or return on investment. There is evidence compatible with wellbeing benefit in small healthcare-worker studies; there is not evidence sufficient for a quantified productivity saving.
### 3.4 Tai chi safety
**MEASURED:** The knee-osteoarthritis review reported no tai chi-related adverse events, and the COPD Cochrane review reported no adverse events in its included studies. Neither finding establishes a population-wide zero-risk rate; both reviews concern supervised trials and selected participants. \[S10\] \[S11\]
## 4. Related traditional practices — do not infer efficacy from cultural grouping or insurance coverage
### 4.1 Moxibustion
**MEASURED — chronic low-back pain:** A 2020 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (987 people) reported favourable pain and function estimates for moxibustion in some comparisons, but rated every result very low to low certainty because of small samples and high risk of bias. Its conclusion was that firm effectiveness conclusions could not be drawn. This is weaker evidence than a claim that moxibustion is established for chronic low-back pain. \[S13\]
**REPORTED — harm:** A systematic review of reported adverse events identified allergic reactions, burns and infections including cellulitis and hepatitis C. Incidence was not established by that case/report literature. \[S21\]
### 4.2 Cupping
**MEASURED — pain:** A 2023 evidence map assessed 14 cupping meta-analyses across five pain-related conditions. AMSTAR-2 ratings were critically low for 36%, low for 50%, moderate for 7% and high for 7%; it found **no high-quality evidence** demonstrating efficacy for pain-related outcomes. Moderate-to-very-low-quality evidence suggests possible pain benefit in some conditions. This is not confirmation of a durable clinical or workplace benefit. \[S14\]
**REPORTED — harm:** A systematic review of Korean study reports found various cupping-related adverse events, described them as rare in the available reports, and concluded that many were avoidable with trained providers. A reliable population incidence rate was not identified. \[S22\]
### 4.3 Chinese herbal medicine and other TCM modalities
**UNKNOWN — universal efficacy:** “Chinese herbal medicine” is a category of many formulations, doses, preparations and target conditions. No credible all-formula, all-condition effect size exists; efficacy must be assessed by formula and indication. This review did not identify high-certainty controlled evidence that Chinese herbal medicines, tui na, gua sha, acupressure, or “TCM” as a whole improve productivity, employee wellbeing, sleep or chronic disease across a general industrial population.
**REPORTED — oral-herbal safety signal and limits:** A systematic review of 227 English-language RCTs estimated 1,765 adverse events in 10,754 people across 112 Chinese-herbal-medicine groups: 10.8% (95% CI 10.3–11.3%). Gastrointestinal events accounted for 33.94%, liver/biliary events for 8.50%. The authors also found 25.1% of RCTs reported no adverse events and 11.3% reported a total but not types, so the estimate may be undercounted and cannot identify causality. \[S15\]
**REPORTED — interpretation:** No insurance coverage, cultural history, traditional rationale, or marketing statement is evidence that a particular herbal product is effective or safe for a particular disease or worker. Formulation-specific quality, contaminants, drug interactions, contraindications and pharmacovigilance remain material considerations. \[S15\] \[S16\]
## 5. Chinese policy and insurance position
**REPORTED — national policy:** China’s *14th Five-Year Plan for TCM Development* calls for development of acupuncture and tui na specialist capacity, non-drug TCM therapies, integration of traditional exercise with rehabilitation, and promotion of tai chi and Baduanjin health-maintenance methods. It also calls for clinical efficacy evaluation research. Policy promotion is not an outcome trial and should not be treated as clinical proof. \[S16\]
**REPORTED — national insurance policy:** The National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) and National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine 2021 guidance directs local systems to include qualifying TCM providers and, subject to rules and local fund capacity, qualifying TCM services and medicines in payment ranges. It encourages inclusion of TCM appropriate technologies with established efficacy and says payment should not impose unreasonable restrictions. It does **not** make every wellness practice automatically reimbursable nationwide. \[S17\]
**REPORTED — Shandong:** The Shandong Healthcare Security Administration stated on 30 March 2026 that therapeutic massage and acupuncture are within medical-insurance fund payment scope and that more than 90% of TCM medical-service items are included; it also states that wellness/health-maintenance items are explicitly outside basic medical insurance. A provincial payment catalogue lists standard acupuncture as eligible (Class A) and several other acupuncture techniques, moxibustion and cupping items as eligible, commonly Class B. Actual reimbursement still depends on the operative catalogue, service code, setting, indication, provider, patient enrolment and payment rules. \[S18\] \[S19\]
**UNKNOWN — local implementation at Dongjiakou:** No verified Qingdao/Dongjiakou provider network, price, eligible code mix, reimbursement rate, waiting time, workforce uptake, or capacity data was found. No cost-saving claim should be made without those data.
## 6. Balanced verdict for quality-of-life and productivity claims
**MEASURED — genuinely supportive:** Tai chi has the strongest identified evidence for falls/balance in older adults and meaningful controlled evidence for selected chronic-condition rehabilitation outcomes; acupuncture has small sham-controlled benefits in chronic pain and episodic migraine prevention. \[S1\] \[S4\] \[S9\] \[S10\] \[S11\]
**MEASURED — equivocal:** Qigong’s self-reported stress, depressive-symptom and sleep results are promising but small, heterogeneous and frequently lack active comparators; moxibustion and cupping pain signals occur within low-certainty evidence. \[S6\] \[S7\] \[S8\] \[S13\] \[S14\]
**UNKNOWN — claims that outrun evidence:** No high-quality controlled evidence identified here supports claims that these modalities increase general workplace productivity, reduce industrial accidents, improve shift-work resilience, create measurable port/industrial cost savings, enhance ecological outcomes, or deliver population-wide chronic-disease prevention. No mechanistic claim about qi was found to be necessary to explain controlled outcomes.
**REPORTED — decision boundary:** A voluntary, culturally appropriate offering can be evaluated as a wellbeing/participation activity only if claims stay within the evidence above. It should not be presented as medical care, productivity infrastructure, an environmental intervention, or a substitute for occupational health, evidence-based clinical services, sleep protection, ergonomic controls, injury prevention, clean air, or work-design improvements.
## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN
- **UNKNOWN:** Controlled effects of acupuncture, qigong, tai chi, moxibustion, cupping, tui na, herbal medicine or combined TCM on Dongjiakou workers’ output, absence, presenteeism, turnover, error rates, injury rates, fatigue-related incidents, or return on investment.
- **UNKNOWN:** Local Qingdao/Dongjiakou service capacity, quality assurance, clinician qualifications, adverse-event reporting, costs, waiting time, uptake, and actual insurance reimbursement by service code.
- **UNKNOWN:** Effects in rotating-shift, high-heat, hazardous-industry, port, steel, chemical-park, data-centre or food-processing populations.
- **UNKNOWN:** Durability beyond the typical short trial follow-up for qigong and workplace wellbeing outcomes, and long-term effects above established exercise, rehabilitation, psychotherapy, sleep interventions or usual care.
- **UNKNOWN:** The share of acupuncture benefit that is attributable uniquely to point-specific needling versus expectation, attention, ritual, movement toward the mean, reporting effects or physiologically active sham procedures.
- **UNKNOWN:** Objective sleep, cognitive performance and safety-critical work outcomes from qigong or tai chi; self-report improvement cannot be converted into such outcomes without measurement.
- **UNKNOWN:** A class-wide efficacy or safety conclusion for “Chinese herbal medicine,” “TCM,” or “traditional practices.” Formula, preparation, indication, dose and co-medication matter.
- **UNKNOWN:** Effects on flora, fauna, water, air, climate, cultural inclusion, AI welfare, or other ecological and social objectives. No evidence for those outcomes was located in this modality review.
