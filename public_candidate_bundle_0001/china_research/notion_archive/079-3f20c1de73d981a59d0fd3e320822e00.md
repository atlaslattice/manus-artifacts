## Executive summary
- **\[SUPPORTED — REPORTED\]** Preventing *crowding* is a defensible health objective. WHO makes a **strong** recommendation to prevent and reduce it, with high-certainty evidence for associations with tuberculosis and diarrhoea, moderate-to-high certainty for other respiratory infections, and weaker evidence for mental health and sleep. WHO does **not** prescribe a universal m²/person threshold. \[S4\] \[S5\]
- **\[PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — REPORTED\]** The latest official national urban benchmark located is **more than 40 m² of housing construction area per urban resident at end-2023**. The 2020 census reported **36.52 m²** for urban family households. A 46 m² target is materially above the 2020 figure, but the 2023 release gives only “\>40”, so the exact current gap is **UNKNOWN**. \[S1\] \[S2\] \[S3\]
- **\[NOT SUPPORTED AS A HEALTH THRESHOLD — REPORTED\]** No controlled or longitudinal evidence located establishes **46 m²/person** as a biological, mental-health, cognitive, sleep, or productivity optimum. It is a reasonable **design ambition**, not an evidence-based cliff below which health fails or above which wellbeing is maximised. \[S4\] \[S5\]
- **\[SUPPORTED, WITH CAUSAL LIMITS — REPORTED\]** Observational and longitudinal studies associate crowding with worse child academic and behavioural outcomes, poorer sleep arrangements, and some mental-health outcomes. Results are heterogeneous, confounded by deprivation, and often null after stronger adjustment; no eligible controlled trial of reducing crowding was found in the WHO review. \[S5\] \[S6\] \[S7\] \[S8\] \[S10\]
- **\[SUPPORTED — REPORTED\]** A per-person m² target alone is inadequate. The literature and standards also distinguish rooms/bedrooms, privacy, household composition, daylight, noise, thermal comfort, usable space, and access to services. Gross floor area can contain walls, circulation, ducts and storage and is not equivalent to usable or private space. \[S4\] \[S11\] \[S13\] \[S14\]
- **\[MIXED EVIDENCE — REPORTED\]** More private dwelling space need not mean low-density sprawl. Higher urban density is associated with transit use and functional walking, while social and health effects are mixed and can become adverse where infrastructure, green space, quiet, affordability, and services do not keep pace. \[S4\] \[S16\]
- **\[SUPPORTED IN DIRECTION, NOT QUANTIFIED LOCALLY — MODELED\]** More new floor area generally entails more construction material and embodied emissions, and more conditioned space can raise operational demand. The added impacts of moving Dongjiakou to 46 m²/person are **UNKNOWN** without population, net-to-gross ratio, building form, material quantities/EPDs, energy code, appliance ownership, operating schedules, and Qingdao weather/energy modelling. \[S15\]
- **\[PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — REPORTED\]** Better housing quality can reduce illness-related absence from work/school in targeted warmth interventions, but direct evidence that dwelling *size* raises worker productivity is weak. Do not present 46 m²/person as a verified productivity investment. \[S17\] \[S18\]
## Decision statement
**\[MIXED EVIDENCE — SYNTHESIS OF REPORTED AND MODELED EVIDENCE\]** Specify **46 m²/person as an aspirational gross-or-net area target only after defining the metric**, and pair it with anti-crowding room/bedroom rules, private/quiet sleep-study space, acoustic and indoor-air performance, affordable energy, universal access, and walkable services. The evidence supports avoiding crowding and poor-quality housing. It does **not** validate 500 ft² as a universal health minimum or optimum.
> **Evidence-class convention.** **MEASURED** = directly observed instrument/survey result; **REPORTED** = statistic, standard, observational study, systematic review, or policy claim reported by its source; **MODELED** = scenario or arithmetic model; **UNKNOWN** = no verified number/evidence located. “Supported” below concerns the stated proposition, not a clinical recommendation.
## 1. China baseline and comparison with the target
**\[REPORTED\]** At the end of 2023, China’s Minister of Housing and Urban-Rural Development stated that **urban per-capita housing construction area exceeded 40 m²**. This is the most recent official national urban figure located. It is an aggregate construction-area statistic; the transcript does not state a decimal value, distribution, median, tenure split, city-level value, usable area, or private area. \[S1\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The 2020 China Census Yearbook reported **36.52 m² per person** for **urban family households**, **46.80 m²** for rural family households, and **41.76 m²** nationally across household types/locations. The official census-yearbook landing page is the primary source; *People’s Daily Overseas* reports the released tabulation and its definitions. These are not interchangeable with the 2023 MHUD urban-resident construction-area series. \[S2\] \[S3\]
**\[MODELED\]** Using 46.0 m², the proposed target is **9.48 m²/person (26.0%) above** the 2020 urban-family-household mean of 36.52 m². It is **4.24 m²/person (10.2%) above** the all-China 2020 household mean of 41.76 m². These are arithmetic comparisons, not wellbeing effect sizes.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The exact difference between 46 m² and the current 2023 national urban average cannot be calculated from “more than 40 m²”. The target could be above, equal to, or below the unreported exact value if that value is respectively below, equal to, or above 46 m².
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No official per-capita housing-area statistic specific to Dongjiakou/Qingdao’s intended residential population was located in this review. No distributional statistic was located for prospective data-centre, port, steel, chemical-park, food-processing, or LNG-terminal workers and their households.
## 2. What outcome evidence actually says about crowding
### 2.1 Physical health: strongest for infection, not a 46 m² dose-response
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO’s systematic review underpinning its guideline identified 21 studies on tuberculosis (TB), 54 on non-TB infectious disease, 13 on gastroenteritis/diarrhoea, 13 on mental health/stress, and three on sleep disorders in its final 2018 synthesis. It judged the certainty that reducing crowding reduces risk as **high for TB and diarrhoea**, **moderate-to-high for non-TB respiratory disease**, **moderate-to-low for mental health**, and **low-to-very-low for sleep disorders**. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** For TB, 11 of 15 threshold studies found a statistically significant association between crowding and TB, while four did not; among four studies of increasing persons per room, only one found a significant increase at 2–4 persons/room versus one person/room, and the exposure-response pattern was not consistently monotonic. The WHO conclusion is therefore strong on the public-health objective of reducing crowding, but it does not identify an m²/person health cutoff. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO found that higher crowding (\>3 or \>4 persons/room in two studies) was associated with more diarrhoea than lower crowding (\<2 or \<4 persons/room), while two other studies did not find a significant difference in diarrhoea cases. Background infection prevalence, water/sanitation, ventilation, and household contact patterns matter; these results should not be treated as an area-only causal estimate for Qingdao. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No controlled study was located that randomised otherwise comparable households to 36, 40, or 46 m²/person and measured infectious, physical, or mental-health outcomes. WHO states that the included evidence was predominantly observational and that its only RCT concerned an intervention unrelated to crowding. \[S5\]
### 2.2 Children: cognition, behaviour and stress
**\[REPORTED\]** In Solari and Mare’s U.S. longitudinal analysis, an additional **person per room** over four years was associated in pooled models with **4.314 fewer mathematics standard points** (*p*=0.006) and **6.762 fewer reading standard points** (*p*=0.001), after demographic and socioeconomic controls. This is observational evidence, not a controlled space intervention. \[S6\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The same study provides an important counterweight: in child fixed-effects models, the mathematics and reading coefficients were **not statistically significant**; the significant within-child result was a **4.2% increase in external behaviour problems** for a one-unit increase in four-year crowding (*p*=0.044). Reported standardized crowding coefficients in the Los Angeles sample were small (about −0.069 mathematics, −0.068 reading, +0.076 external behaviour, −0.048 physical health, and −0.09 internal behaviour). This is evidence of possible harm, not proof of a large causal educational effect. \[S6\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO’s review found eight of 13 mental-health/stress studies reported at least one significant adverse association, but four cross-sectional studies and two cohort/retrospective studies found no relationship for particular outcomes, including hyperactivity/emotional symptoms, psychological distress, suicidal ideation, self-esteem, schizophrenia, autonomic reactivity, or externalising behaviour. It therefore rates the crowding–mental-health evidence moderate-to-low and calls for studies of child intellectual development and exposure-response. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** In a 2024 Jamaican sample of 1,311 four-year-olds, crowding was associated with worse locomotor and personal-social development, while language, coordination, performance and practical-reasoning domains showed **no significant association**. The abstract does not provide coefficients; their magnitude is **UNKNOWN** from the accessible source. \[S10\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No controlled evidence was located showing that giving children exactly 46 m²/person, rather than another non-crowded arrangement with a separate quiet study/sleep area, improves cortisol, cognition, educational attainment, or stress. A plausible mechanism—less interruption, conflict and noise and more privacy—has outcome associations but is not independently proven at this numeric target. \[S4\] \[S5\] \[S6\]
### 2.3 Sleep: evidence exists, but it is sparse and not a floor-area threshold
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO found only two cross-sectional and one ecological sleep study. One cross-sectional study associated \>1 person/room with excessive daytime sleepiness; another found no significant association with most sleep-disturbance outcomes but did find an association with sleep duration in some analyses. WHO rated the evidence that reducing crowding reduces sleep disorders **low to very low**. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** In a cross-sectional U.S. study of 120 low-income preschool children, parent-room sharing correlated with worse sleep quality (**Spearman ρ=0.28, p\<0.01**). Sleeping alone in one’s own bed was associated with better sleep quality in a stepwise model (**β=−0.24, SE=0.47, R²=0.06, p=0.01**). It tests sleeping arrangements, not m²/person, and cannot establish causation or apply a Western individual-sleep norm universally. \[S8\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A 2025 study using the 2022 China Family Panel Studies (25 provinces/municipalities) reported that urban housing crowding was associated with a lower likelihood of normal sleep duration after covariate adjustment, with subjective wellbeing and perceived social status as partial mediators. The accessible abstract/table preview does not provide a numerical coefficient, so its effect size is **UNKNOWN** here. This is locally relevant but cross-sectional evidence. \[S9\]
### 2.4 Adult mental health: association, confounding and contested direction
**\[REPORTED\]** A 2018 cross-sectional survey of 681 recently arrived migrants in Sweden found crowded living associated with mental ill-health: unadjusted **OR 1.46 (95% CI 1.05–2.03)** and age/education-adjusted **OR 1.47 (1.05–2.07)**. After adding housing stability, the association was not conventionally statistically significant: **OR 1.40 (0.99–1.99), p=0.061**. This illustrates both a possible adverse effect and substantial confounding by housing insecurity. \[S7\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO reports at least one counterintuitive cross-sectional finding—lower psychiatric disability among crowded Chilean households—and non-monotonic findings in which both very uncrowded and very crowded homes were associated with depression in one cohort. The popular claim that “more area always causes better mental health” is therefore stronger than the evidence permits. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A Chinese observational study using the 2011 China Household Finance Survey reported that number of rooms, per-capita **usable** area and years of use were positively associated with urban residents’ subjective happiness, while property/price effects were not significant at the 10% level in its analysis. This is China-specific evidence of association, but it does not show an optimum, effect size in the accessible abstract, causation, or a 46 m² threshold. \[S19\]
## 3. Minimums, standards and the absence of an optimum
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO’s health guideline makes a strong recommendation to develop and implement strategies to prevent/reduce household crowding, but explicitly asks each Member State to select an appropriate **measure and threshold**. It lists incompatible measures: UN-Habitat (\>3 people/habitable room), an American index (\>1 person/room; severe \>1.5), Canadian/UK bedroom norms, Eurostat room norms, and some studies using m²/person. This is a strong anti-crowding recommendation—not a WHO 46 m²/person recommendation. \[S4\] \[S5\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The English Nationally Described Space Standard (NDSS) sets a **minimum 39 m² Gross Internal Area (GIA)** for a one-bedroom/one-person, one-storey dwelling, reducible to **37 m²** when it has a shower room rather than a bathroom. It is statutory planning guidance for new dwellings where adopted, not a medical threshold or an optimum. The same standard sets bedroom, storage, width and headroom requirements rather than a flat m²/person ratio. \[S13\]
**\[REPORTED\]** China’s historical *Design Code for Residential Buildings* GB 50096-2011 set **22 m² usable area** for a minimum unit comprising a combined living/sleeping room, kitchen and bathroom, and **30 m² usable area** for a unit with bedroom, living room, kitchen and bathroom. These are unit-type minima, not per-person health standards. The 2025 *Residential Project Code* GB 55038-2025 is compulsory from 1 May 2025 and supersedes listed earlier mandatory provisions; this review did not verify a current Chinese code provision that establishes a national m²/person health minimum. \[S11\] \[S12\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO cites UNHCR emergency-shelter guidance of **4.5–5.5 m² per evacuee in cold climates**. This is an emergency shelter benchmark and cannot credibly be used as a normal, long-term wellbeing, privacy, child-development, or flourishing benchmark. \[S4\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No public-health body or systematic review located proposed 46 m²/person as a universal long-term minimum or optimum. The numerical rules found have different purposes: emergency survival, overcrowding surveillance, bedroom allocation, building-plan compliance, and unit design. They should not be merged into a single “scientific optimum.”
## 4. Floor area is not usable space, private space, or crowding
**\[REPORTED\]** Under GB 50096-2011, “usable area” is the actual usable room area and excludes wall/column structural area; unit usable area aggregates rooms, kitchen, bathroom, hall, corridor, storage and cupboards. Under the English NDSS, GIA is measured between internal perimeter walls and **includes** partitions, structural elements, cupboards, ducts, stairs and stair voids. A target called “46 m²/person” is uninterpretable until it states which measure applies. \[S11\] \[S13\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Eurostat defines overcrowding by household composition and **number of rooms**, e.g., a room for the household, couple, each adult single person, and specified child pairings. WHO’s crowding evidence likewise uses persons/room, rooms/house, room type, and sometimes square meterage. These measures can classify two dwellings with the same m²/person differently. \[S4\] \[S14\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No universally accepted conversion was located between gross construction area, net usable area, habitable-room area, bedroom area, and *private* personal area. Shared kitchens/living rooms may improve sociability and reduce material demand, but they cannot substitute for sufficient privacy, sleep separation, quiet study/work space, accessibility, storage and resident control.
**\[REPORTED\]** The Chinese standard’s explicit separate requirements for bedroom, living room, kitchen, bathroom, daylight, ventilation, noise and indoor air quality demonstrate why a single total-area number is incomplete as a quality standard. This is a design-code inference, not outcome evidence that every listed component has the same wellbeing effect size. \[S11\]
## 5. Per-person space, density, walkability, services and social connection
**\[REPORTED\]** Dwelling floor area per person and urban population density are different denominators. A 46 m²/person allocation can be delivered in compact mid/high-rise buildings or dispersed low-rise buildings; without site area, floor-area ratio, household composition, common-area ratio, building height, employment locations and transport/service network, Dongjiakou persons/km² and walkability are **UNKNOWN**.
**\[REPORTED\]** A systematic review of 229 quantitative empirical studies (31% Asian, 29% North American, 22% European) found broad agreement that higher density is associated with more public transport and active travel and less car ownership/vehicle miles, but reported mixed or adverse environmental, social and some health outcomes. Social-interaction claims used to justify density were more positive in planning documents than in the empirical studies reviewed. This is a large observational evidence base, not a universal density prescription. \[S16\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Within the density review, one study reported that walking-trip odds increased with residential density but the effect declined at roughly **10,000–15,000 persons/km²**; in the same study, residents in the lowest-density areas were **3.38 times** more likely to take the car, with the association becoming insignificant around 20,000 persons/km². These are study-specific associations, not transferable “optimal density” thresholds for Qingdao. \[S16\]
**\[REPORTED\]** WHO cautions that reducing crowding by relocation can harm health and earnings if it breaks social networks, childcare, work or education access, and that low-density/sprawling new housing can reduce physical activity. The appropriate standard is therefore *adequate private dwelling space plus accessible services and social infrastructure*, not area maximisation alone. \[S4\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No Dongjiakou-specific evidence was located on residents’ existing social ties, travel time, school capacity, healthcare access, public/open-space use, disability access, cultural participation, perceived safety, or the density range at which service access and social connection improve or worsen.
## 6. Construction materials and energy at locality scale
**\[MODELED\]** If 46 m²/person denotes residential **gross** floor area and the locality population is **P**, direct dwelling floor area is **46P m²**. If it denotes net usable area, gross floor area is larger by an unknown net-to-gross factor. Material mass, upfront embodied carbon and operating energy cannot be calculated without a material-intensity/life-cycle dataset and an operational-energy model.
**\[REPORTED\]** The IPCC assesses reduced per-capita floor-area growth as a “sufficiency” measure: less new floor area avoids construction materials and heating, cooling, lighting, appliance and equipment demand; embodied emissions can be lowered by limiting new floor area and by reducing material mass/intensity. It does not say that a given 46 m²/person target has a fixed impact in every climate or building type. \[S15\]
**\[MODELED\]** In an IPCC-cited multi-country scenario, a 20% reduction in efficient use of living space was associated with **56–58% lower 2050 material-production GHG emissions** and **9–10% lower heating/cooling demand** versus its baseline. This is a scenario result combining assumptions and technology pathways; it is not an observed marginal effect of moving a Chinese locality from 40 to 46 m²/person. \[S15\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The following inputs are required before claiming a Dongjiakou gain, saving, carbon cost, water cost or electricity cost from 46 m²/person: resident count and household mix; target metric (GIA/net/private); common-space allocation; building height/form; structure and façade quantities; product-specific EPDs; construction logistics; service life/reuse; cooling/heating/ventilation design; renewable supply; plug loads; resident schedules; tariffs; and climate-resilient design loads. No verified locality-scale bill of quantities or energy model was located.
## 7. Housing quality and productivity
**\[REPORTED\]** A Cochrane systematic review found that targeted warmth/energy-efficiency housing improvements could improve health, usable space, privacy and social relationships. In one New Zealand RCT, adults receiving warmth improvements had fewer illness-related days off work (**adjusted incidence-rate ratio 0.62; 95% CI 0.466–0.82**); school absence effects in two RCTs were also lower (reported effect ratios 0.79 and adjusted IRR 0.81). These are controlled intervention results on thermal housing quality, not on larger dwelling area. \[S17\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The same review says socio-economic evidence was sparse and heterogeneous, with too little suitable data for meta-analysis. It describes a *suggestion* that adequate, affordable warmth may reduce absence from school/work; it does not establish that increasing space from 36–40 to 46 m²/person improves output per worker. \[S17\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A 2022 scoping review associated inadequate size/flexibility and poor spatial design with lower reported productivity/cognitive fatigue, and adequate visual comfort with higher reported productivity. Its authors state important limitations: one database, English-only 2012–June 2022 literature, heterogeneous grouped outcomes and no meta-analysis. This is useful mechanism-level evidence, not a robust causal productivity effect size. \[S18\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** No controlled study or credible local observational estimate was located for the effect of 46 m²/person on absenteeism, presenteeism, workplace output, data-centre/industrial worker retention, school attainment, or unpaid care productivity in Dongjiakou.
