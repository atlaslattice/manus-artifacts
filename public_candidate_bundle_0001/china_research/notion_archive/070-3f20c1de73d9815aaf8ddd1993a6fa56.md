## Executive summary
- **REPORTED.** A modern direct-liquid-cooled (DLC) GPU/HPC installation does **not** universally require 15–25°C coolant. Open Compute Project (OCP) deployment groups span **15–25°C**, **30–37°C**, and **40–45°C** secondary supply; the latter two are explicitly used to reduce or avoid chiller operation. \[1\] \[2\]
- **REPORTED (vendor specification; not independently audited).** Lenovo's current Neptune system with four NVIDIA H200 GPUs per tray permits water inlet temperatures up to **45°C**, subject to configuration. This is strong evidence that high-density cold plates can use *warm* water, not evidence that every GPU platform can. \[5\]
- **MEASURED / REPORTED.** Real operating examples show a wide PUE spread: an academic field study measured **2.33** at a mechanically air-cooled, air-cooled-chiller data centre, while NREL and LRZ report **1.06** for chiller-less/direct warm-water HPC facilities. These are not a controlled comparison and do **not** establish a 1.06 PUE for Dongjiakou. \[8\] \[9\] \[10\]
- **REPORTED.** A −5 to +5°C industrial stream should **not** be sent straight to server cold plates. A liquid-to-liquid heat exchanger / coolant-distribution unit (CDU) normally isolates the technology cooling system (TCS) from facility water, controls TCS temperature, pressure and chemistry, and keeps TCS supply at least 2°C above room dew point. \[3\] \[4\]
- **MODELED.** For a deliberately transparent 40 MW heat-duty sensitivity case using a −5/+15°C primary stream, a 0.60 m-ID twin pipe, water-equivalent properties and 75% pump efficiency, *straight-pipe friction only* is **20 / 40 / 121 kW** at **0.5 / 1 / 3 km** one-way separation. This is not a Dongjiakou design result; actual brine/glycol properties, fittings, elevation, redundancy and pipe diameter are unknown.
- **MODELED.** With an assumed insulated-pipe conductance of 0.80 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ and 25°C ground, primary-loop heat gain is **16 / 32 / 96 kW** at 0.5 / 1 / 3 km. Under those assumptions it is small relative to 40 MW, but the assumption has not been validated for the route or carrier fluid.
- **UNKNOWN.** No publicly verifiable operating data centre was found that is demonstrably cooled by an industrial LNG/process waste-cold stream and reports measured annual PUE, cooling electricity, availability, or outage performance. Located LNG/data-centre material is modelling, research/prototype, or a project launch rather than operating evidence. \[11\] \[12\] \[13\] \[14\]
- **REPORTED / UNKNOWN.** External cold is a reliability and control dependency, not automatically a substitute for redundant heat rejection. Liquid-cooling guidance calls for leak monitoring and redundant pumps/CDUs; the availability, temperature stability, trip logic, storage and backup cooling of the Qingdao source are **UNKNOWN**. \[1\] \[3\] \[4\]
## Evidence-class convention
**MEASURED** means an instrumented result reported by a study or facility. **REPORTED** means a source states a fact but underlying raw measurements were not independently audited here. **MODELED** means a calculation or simulation result, with its assumptions stated. **UNKNOWN** means no defensible number or verified evidence was found; it is not filled by inference.
## Direct answer
**MODELED (evidence-based assessment).** A 40 MW IT data centre can use an external cold source *as a facility-side heat sink* and thereby avoid or sharply reduce mechanical refrigeration **if** the source is sufficiently firm and a properly sized interface, distribution system and backup heat-rejection system are provided. It should not treat −5 to +5°C industrial carrier fluid as server coolant. The defensible architecture is: industrial cold loop → isolated liquid-to-liquid heat exchanger / CDU (with bypass and controls) → treated secondary TCS loop → cold plates, plus residual-air cooling and redundant backup heat rejection. This conclusion is **PARTIALLY CONFIRMED**, not fully confirmed for Dongjiakou, because the source-side duty profile, fluid chemistry, route, pipe design, availability SLA and backup configuration are all **UNKNOWN**.
## 1. What coolant temperature does high-density DLC actually need?
**REPORTED.** The commonly quoted 15–25°C band is an OCP **low-temperature secondary-supply group**, chosen to maximize cooling capability and described as needing heavy chiller/evaporative-cooling use. OCP also documents a **30–37°C medium** group and a **40–45°C high** group. The latter is intended to maximize efficiency and can be supported by liquid-to-air hybrid equipment and low/no chiller use depending on site conditions. Thus 15–25°C is a system choice, not a universal cold-plate requirement. \[1\]
**REPORTED.** ASHRAE's liquid-cooling classes express the *maximum facility-water supply* as W17, W27, W32, W40, W45 and W+; W32/W40 are typically operated without chillers in many locations, and W45/W+ are typically operated without chillers. Its published lower limit for all water classes is **2°C**. This means that a −5°C *water* feed is outside the stated class boundary; a sub-zero primary stream would normally be a glycol/brine/process loop behind an exchanger, not water admitted to IT equipment. \[2\]
**REPORTED (vendor specification; not independently audited).** The Lenovo ThinkSystem SD650-N V3 Neptune direct-water-cooled platform uses four NVIDIA H200 SXM5 GPUs per tray. The product specification permits up to **45°C** inlet water (with stated configuration exceptions), and lists 5 L min⁻¹ per tray at that condition. Lenovo also reports customer-site heat capture of 98% at 45°C inlet for an earlier V2 installation; that heat-capture result is an interested-party claim and is **not** used here as independent performance evidence. \[5\]
**REPORTED.** OCP's liquid-cooling guidance uses a typical coolant temperature rise of 7.5–12°C and identifies a 10°C design target. This is relevant to flow sizing, but it does not prescribe a universal rack inlet temperature. \[1\]
### Implication for the proposed cascade
**MODELED.** A practical cold-grade match is not “−5°C into the rack.” A primary industrial carrier could arrive at −5°C and leave warmer, while a controlled TCS is supplied at a hardware-approved setpoint such as 17–25°C, 30–37°C, or warmer if the selected rack supports it. The exchanger needs a finite approach temperature; therefore final supply temperature, source return temperature and heat-exchanger area must be designed together. Neither the selected server SKU nor the temperature approach is available for Dongjiakou, so the actual TCS setpoint is **UNKNOWN**.
**REPORTED.** The TCS lower bound is dynamic because it depends on room dew point. ASHRAE's water-cooled-server guidance says the CDU should raise TCS supply to at least **2°C above room dew point**; the OCP CDU guide states the same requirement. This is a direct condensation-control reason not to expose cold plates, manifolds, hoses and racks to an uncontrolled −5 to +5°C feed. \[3\] \[4\]
## 2. Does the external cold stream need an intermediate loop?
**REPORTED.** A liquid-to-liquid CDU isolates the facility-water system (FWS) from the technology-cooling system (TCS), transfers heat through a heat exchanger, and controls TCS flow, pressure and temperature. OCP describes this as a key component of DLC. \[3\]
**REPORTED.** ASHRAE lists the CDU functions as transferring heat from TCS to FWS, circulating TCS coolant, preventing condensation by controlling TCS temperature above room dew point, preserving a TCS chemistry different from FWS, and providing flexible coolant temperature. It warns that applying facility-water quality to fine-pitch cold plates can lead to plugging, corrosion or failure. \[4\]
**MODELED (design conclusion).** For this project, an **intermediate loop is the default required interface**, because the proposed cold stream is industrial, potentially sub-zero, and its chemistry, pressure, solids, biofouling risk and transients are **UNKNOWN**. The recommended boundary is a plate heat exchanger/CDU between the industrial cold loop and a treated, closed TCS loop. A direct connection could only be considered after the external fluid, water quality, pressure envelope, temperature controls, server vendor warranty and condensation control have been demonstrated compatible. No such evidence was found for Dongjiakou.
**REPORTED.** Liquid cooling has material reliability consequences: OCP notes failures can be more severe than air-cooling failures and identifies leak detection with CDU/pump response as a typical deployment approach. Lenovo's reference architecture calls for redundant secondary pumps, temperature/pressure sensors, redundant power feeds, isolation valves, filtration and leak detection. These are vendor/industry guidance, not evidence of the proposed site's redundancy. \[1\] \[6\]
## 3. PUE evidence: direct liquid cooling versus mechanical air/refrigeration cooling
**REPORTED.** PUE is total facility energy divided by IT energy. It is valuable as a facility-overhead metric, but it is not a controlled measure of cooling technology alone: IT load, power distribution, climate, heat-reuse boundary, utilisation, measurement period and cooling topology all change it. \[7\]

**MEASURED / REPORTED (synthesis).** The operating evidence supports the proposition that warm-water DLC can achieve PUE around 1.06 at particular HPC sites, while measured mechanically cooled air sites in the cited field study range from 1.37 to 2.33. It does **not** support a universal liquid-versus-air PUE delta, an LNG-cold PUE, or a 40 MW Dongjiakou guarantee. \[8\] \[9\] \[10\]
**MODELED (metering-boundary implication).** If terminal-side pumps, heat exchangers or a cold-energy plant are electrically metered outside the data-centre facility boundary, PUE can appear to improve by moving energy outside the numerator. Any project comparison should therefore meter and report: (a) IT power, (b) all on-site cooling and distribution power, and (c) terminal-side incremental electricity. This follows directly from the facility-energy definition of PUE; it is a proposed accounting treatment, not a measured site result. \[7\]

## 6. Is there an operating industrial-waste-cold data centre?
**MODELED.** Sermsuk et al. simulate an LNG receiving terminal coupled to a data centre in Aspen HYSYS. The study validates an intermediate-fluid vaporiser model against terminal data, but the data-centre integration, electricity comparison and PUE are simulation outputs, not operating-data-centre measurements. It is useful process feasibility evidence, not commercial proof. \[11\]
**REPORTED (research project).** Keppel, NUS and Singapore LNG describe ICE-SEED as fundamental research and prototype development for carrying LNG cold energy to a data centre. The page says the PUE reduction is an estimate. It is explicitly laboratory/prototype-stage work, not commercial operation. \[12\]
**REPORTED (journalism, proposal).** IEEE Spectrum reported in 2014 that TeraCool had proposed LNG/data-centre heat-exchange loops and had not found an operator willing to try the system at that time. No later operating performance data were located in this research. \[13\]
**REPORTED (project announcement; interested parties).** Zhuhai's March 2025 announcement records a cooperation agreement for an LNG green intelligent-computing centre, says first-phase commissioning was *expected* in Q3 2025, and claims cooling-energy reduction above 50%. This is a launch/schedule and promotional claim, not evidence that the plant was commissioned or that it produced measured PUE, cooling energy or availability data. \[14\]
**UNKNOWN.** On the evidence reviewed, no operating site can be cited as a verified global precedent for an industrial LNG/process-waste-cold-cooled data centre with measured annual results. This is a documented search result, not proof that no such installation exists.

- **No independent measurement dataset** was found validating the LNG-to-data-centre PUE/cooling-electricity reductions claimed in the HYSYS study, Keppel research, TeraCool proposal, or Zhuhai project announcement.

- **No controlled 40 MW comparison study** was found that isolates direct liquid cooling from mechanical refrigeration while holding IT hardware, climate, load factor, power chain, measurement boundary and annual period constant.
