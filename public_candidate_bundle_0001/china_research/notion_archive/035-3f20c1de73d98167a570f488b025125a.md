## 1. Yes — and it corrects something more basic than an addition rule
It makes sense, and I think it is the correct accounting. But it changes more than how we add things up. **It changes the unit of account.**
The register has been evaluating products **standalone** — "what does a litre of lithium cost?" — and reporting the answer as though it settled the question. That is the wrong unit. The right unit is the node, for two reasons the argument supplies:
**First, the shared step is incurred once.** The mineral train concentrates the brine to NaCl saturation. That step — 301.6 GWh/yr of LSRRO, the largest energy term in the whole budget — is incurred **once**, and every product downstream uses it: NaCl, KCl, Mg, Br, Li, Sr. The marginal cost of lithium is therefore **not the cost of a lithium plant.** It is the cost of the selective sorbent step bolted onto a train that already exists and is already paid for. Evaluating lithium standalone charges it for a concentration plant it does not need and would never have been built for it alone.
**Second, if the node is net positive, the marginal input is surplus.** This is the deeper point, and it is the one I had not properly internalised. A net-positive node has, by definition, more energy, more water and more removal capacity than it consumes. **Surplus input has a marginal cost near zero.** So the mineral train is not competing for scarce resources against the compute load — it is consuming what is left over. That is what makes the whole exchange work.
**So the register was wrong, and the correction is three-part:**
1. **Unit of account = the node**, not the product.
2. **Shared steps are charged once, to the train**, never to each product.
3. **Each product is evaluated as an increment** to a train that is already viable.

**If a surplus is allowed to offset a deficit through a money rate, the register has re-imported the error it exists to prevent** — valuing the node by an exchange rate instead of by physical production. The bromine lesson was exactly this: a valuation that looked fine in currency and dissolved in physical terms.
The physical chains are real and they are plentiful:
```javascript
ENERGY    → MATERIALS   surplus electricity drives concentration and crystallisation
ENERGY    → CARBON      surplus electricity powers capture, grinding or mineralisation
ENERGY    → WATER       surplus electricity drives RO or thermal distillation
WATER     → MATERIALS   surplus product water becomes a mineral-train reagent
MATERIALS → CARBON      Mg/Ca cations consumed as carbon-mineralisation feedstock
MATERIALS → ECOLOGICAL  removed mass is mass out of the receiving environment
CARBON    → MATERIALS   captured CO2 is a reactant in carbonate and cement chemistry
ECOLOGICAL→ CARBON      restored biomass fixes carbon
```
**So the offsetting is legitimate exactly where the user says it is** — and the mechanism is that the surplus is consumed, not that it is exchanged. That is a much stronger claim, and it is the one the register can defend.

## 4. What must not be relaxed: per-metric net positivity
The user already conceded this ("looking granularity at each metric is important"), and it needs stating because the offsetting logic creates an obvious temptation.
**A node that is +1,000 GWh of energy and −5,000 m³ of water is not net positive.**
Surplus energy cannot repair a water deficit. There is no physical chain from ENERGY to WATER that *removes a water deficit* — the chain runs the other way (energy can *produce* water, which is why `ENERGY → WATER` is on the list, but only when the energy is actually routed to desalination or thermal distillation, and then the water metric moves by the amount produced, not by an "offset").
**So the rule is: every metric clears zero, and the aggregate score never appears.** An aggregate would hide precisely the failure mode this whole programme exists to avoid — a node that looks net positive and is quietly draining one resource.
And note the ordering in the metric list: **FINANCIAL is last.** Currency is the metric to consult *after* the physical ones, never the one that adjudicates them.

## 5. The horizon condition
The argument is explicitly long-horizon, and the register should record that as a condition rather than an assumption.
**"Near zero marginal cost" is true only once cumulative surplus has covered the capital.** An unamortised capex is a real cost, so the claim carries a horizon:
```javascript
years               — the horizon over which the claim is made
annual_net_surplus  — the surplus per year
capex               — the capital to recover
cumulative_surplus  — surplus x years
capex_recovered     — bool
years_to_recover    — capex / annual_net_surplus
```
**I would make it a register rule that the horizon must be stated whenever "near zero marginal cost" is claimed.** A figure asserted without a horizon is a first-year figure wearing a long-run label.

## 6. The honest condition on all of it
**The accounting rule is correct. Its premise is still a hypothesis.**
Everything above is conditional on the node **actually being net positive** — and that is the programme's *goal*, not an established result. Today the register holds:
- an energy **deficit** of \~24 GWh/yr before any mineral exists (714 GWh/yr train against 690 GWh/yr generation);
- **0 credit** realised;
- **6 of 6 vetoes UNRESOLVED**;
- and every mineral figure a **dissolved-inventory ceiling** (100% capture, before process loss).
**So the corrected accounting does not create the surplus — it tells us what the surplus would be worth once it exists.** If the heat-integration study succeeds and the node's physical balance goes positive, then the incremental logic above becomes operative and lithium genuinely costs the sorbent step and nothing more. If it does not, the incremental logic is still the *right* way to account, but there is no surplus for it to distribute, and the train must be sized to the free energy.
**That is the honest sequencing: fix the balance, then the allocation rule pays out.** The argument is right, and it is right in advance of the condition being met.
