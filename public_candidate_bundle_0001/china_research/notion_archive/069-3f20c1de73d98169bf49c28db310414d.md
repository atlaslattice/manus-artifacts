## Executive summary
- **REPORTED — China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE) says 2023 construction-waste generation exceeded 3.0 billion tonnes and current resource utilisation was only 10–30%.** This is the best nationwide official figure found, but it is in an explanatory note to a *draft* standard rather than an audited National Bureau of Statistics series; it must not be treated as a measured Dongjiakou supply figure. \[1\]
- **MEASURED — Clean, crushed concrete and brick fractions can be formulated into printable mortar/concrete after selective demolition, removal of contaminants, crushing and tight particle grading; this is confirmed only at laboratory scale, not commercial building scale.** Unsorted C&D waste is not printable feedstock. \[2\] \[3\] \[4\]
- **MEASURED (laboratory studies) — Recycled fine aggregate commonly raises water demand, viscosity/yield stress and buildability while reducing flow/open time; higher replacement can reduce compressive and interlayer strength.** Reported usable replacement levels range widely by mix and equipment; there is no universally validated “C&D percentage” for a Dongjiakou mix. \[3\] \[4\] \[5\]
- **UNKNOWN / documented null — No independently documented, occupied building-scale 3D-printed project using recycled C&D aggregate was found in China or internationally in the reviewed primary/peer-reviewed sources.** The strongest directly evidenced recycled-C&D cases are laboratory demonstrations: printed coupons/columns/slabs, not houses. \[5\] \[6\]
- **REPORTED — China has real occupied 3D-printed homes, but the available project record for the 2021 Wujiazhuang, Hebei house specifies polypropylene-fibre concrete, not recycled C&D aggregate.** It cannot be cited as proof of C&D-to-housing deployment. \[7\] \[8\]
- **REPORTED — China has current recycled-aggregate standards, including GB/T 25176-2010 for recycled fine aggregate, but the 2024 document specifically addressing 3D-printed concrete components was still an association-standard consultation draft.** It is not evidence of an issued national 3D-printed-building code or automatic approval of a recycled C&D mix. \[9\] \[10\]
- **UNKNOWN — A defensible China-wide cost comparison in RMB/m² for completed 3D-printed housing versus conventional housing was not found.** The only located Chinese installed-house figure was media-reported at ¥200,000 for a 106 m² Wujiazhuang house (reported as ¥2,000–2,500/m²) and said to be above local rural self-build cost; scopes are not comparable. Vendor claims of 50% savings are interested-party assertions. \[8\] \[11\] \[12\]
- **MODELED — Decision verdict: PARTIALLY CONFIRMED.** A segregated, quality-controlled mineral C&D stream is technically credible for *R&D/pilot printable mix development*. It does **not** presently justify an assumption of commercial C&D-fed 3D-printed housing, a fixed recycled-content ratio, a local feedstock yield, or a cost advantage at Dongjiakou. \[3\] \[5\] \[13\]
## Evidence code and decision rule
**MEASURED** means a source reports a physical test, measurement or observed project attribute. **REPORTED** means an official, academic, media or project-source statement that was not independently audited here. **MODELED** means an LCA, cost calculation, projection or transfer of published data to this decision. **UNKNOWN** means no supported value or verification was found; it is not an invitation to estimate.
> **Decision rule — MODELED:** For this node, C&D should be considered only as a *segregated mineral raw-material candidate* for a staged materials programme. Treat feedstock quantity, composition, recycled-content ratio, structural approval, offtake and project economics as **UNKNOWN** until locally sampled, trial-mixed, independently tested and permitted.
## 1. China’s C&D-waste quantity and routes
### 1.1 Nationwide quantity
**REPORTED — The MEE’s April 2025 explanatory note for the draft *Technical Specification for Construction Waste Pollution Control* states that China generated more than 3.0 billion tonnes of construction waste in 2023, more than 40% of urban waste by mass.** The note defines the stream broadly: engineering spoil, engineering slurry, construction waste, demolition waste and renovation waste. This is an official regulator statement, but the note does not publish a city-by-city source dataset, weighbridge method, uncertainty range or a Dongjiakou/Qingdao number. It is therefore **not MEASURED** supply to the proposed node. \[1\]
**REPORTED — The same MEE note gives a current national resource-utilisation range of 10–30%, chiefly engineering backfill and recycled-product use.** It explicitly contrasts that range with 80–90% in developed countries. The broad 10–30% range conflicts with older academic estimates: a 2022 peer-reviewed study, citing NDRC 2014 statistics, reported 1.0 billion tonnes generated and 50 million tonnes recycled (5%); another 2022 study cited approximately 2.4 billion tonnes annually and 3–10% utilisation. These are different years, definitions and underlying datasets—not a valid time series. \[1\] \[14\] \[15\]

### 1.2 Disposal routes, including landfill and illegal dumping
**REPORTED — MEE identifies illegal dumping, arbitrary burial, poorly managed stockpiles, cross-provincial dumping, mixing with municipal solid waste and inadequate collection/transport systems as recurrent problems.** It does not quantify the national share that is illegally dumped. \[1\]
**REPORTED — The State Council’s 2025 circular requires classified treatment and states that engineering spoil and dewatered slurry may be used for earthworks, levelling, roads, environmental remediation or fired products; construction, demolition and renovation wastes should preferentially be used for recycled aggregate, recycled building materials and road materials.** Material that cannot be used is to receive harmless disposal. The same circular requires planning of long-term C&D landfill capacity alongside recycling facilities, so landfill remains a residual route rather than a route demonstrated to have disappeared. \[2\]
**REPORTED — The MEE explanatory note says sorted concrete, brick/tile, waste mortar and waste cement products from construction, demolition and renovation waste can be used for recycled aggregate, recycled cement products and road-base materials; separated plastic should preferentially be recycled and combustible wood-like debris may be incinerated or pyrolysed.** This establishes intended/allowed route categories, not actual tonnages. \[1\]
**REPORTED — A 2022 peer-reviewed policy study reported that direct dumping was still the major disposal measure in China, attributing the finding to Chen et al. (2019), and linked poor recycling incentives to low landfill charges and high recycling costs.** This supports the history of unmanaged disposal but is not a 2023–2026 measured national routing statistic. \[14\]
### 1.3 Composition: what China officially classifies, and what could become a printing material
**REPORTED — China’s official 2025 classification is by source/type, not a national mass composition table: engineering spoil, slurry, construction waste, demolition waste and renovation waste.** The official sources reviewed do not provide nationwide percentages for concrete, brick, tile, gypsum, wood, plastic or metal. \[1\] \[2\]
**UNKNOWN — A nationally representative, official China mass-composition dataset for the requested individual fractions was not found.** Composition changes materially by city, building age, whether excavated soil is included, selective versus mixed demolition, and renovation content. A percentage in a city case study would not be a defensible input for Dongjiakou without local sampling.

## 2. What 3D-printable recycled C&D feedstock actually requires
### 2.1 It is a manufactured, quality-controlled input—not landfill material
**REPORTED — RCA production described in the literature requires selective demolition, two-stage mechanical crushing and separation of reinforcing steel, wood and plastics.** The resulting material is classified as coarse 5–32 mm and fine below 5 mm; the fine fraction is especially relevant to normal 3D concrete printing because of pump/nozzle constraints. \[3\]
**REPORTED — The 2024 Chinese association draft for 3D-printed concrete components says fine aggregate may be river sand, manufactured sand or recycled solid-waste raw material; it requires hard, well-graded fine aggregate, while coarse aggregate should be hard and well graded, compatible with the pump/extrusion system and normally no larger than 16 mm.** This is a consultation draft, not an enacted national construction standard. \[10\]
**MODELED — For the Dongjiakou case, a credible material flow would be: source-separated concrete/masonry → contaminant removal → crush/screen/classify → wash or otherwise control fines/soluble contaminants where laboratory results require it → batch-specific moisture/absorption correction → purpose-designed printable mix → printed-material and structure qualification.** No source establishes that a generic local landfill or mixed transfer-station stream can omit these steps. \[1\] \[3\] \[10\]
### 2.2 Rheology, grading, water absorption and buildability
**MEASURED — In 3D printing, the mixture must be pumpable, extrudable, buildable and able to bond between layers; these are competing requirements.** The 2024 Chinese draft defines printability as continuous, uniform extrusion, shape stability during stacking and good interlayer bonding. \[10\]
**MEASURED — In a 2021 systematic review, raising sand-to-binder ratio from 0.6 to 1.2 increased plastic viscosity by 16.4% and yield stress by 129.8%; increasing it from 1.0 to 1.4 increased yield stress from 0.67 to 0.82 kPa and viscosity from 17.1 to 43.1 Pa·s in cited experiments.** This shows why aggregate grading and content cannot be chosen from a waste-diversion target alone. \[4\]
**MEASURED — Recycled fine aggregate (FRCA) increases static yield stress and plastic viscosity because of surface area, porosity and adhered mortar.** At 50% replacement, the review reports reduced flowability and shorter open time, although structural build-up and interlayer stability improved; superplasticiser use was needed to preserve nozzle flow. \[3\]
**MEASURED — A controlled 2025 Polish laboratory study used pure C30/37 ready-mix test-specimen waste, not mixed demolition waste.** It crushed and sieved this to 0.125–2.0 mm RFA and 0–0.125 mm powder; its RFA water absorption was 6.85 percentage points higher than the natural fine aggregate. This establishes an absorption-management issue under unusually clean feed conditions—not an expected value for Dongjiakou waste. \[5\]
**MEASURED — In a 2025 ISARC laboratory study, 100% replacement of natural fine aggregate with waste-concrete fine aggregate required raising water-to-binder ratio from 0.20 to 0.25 plus a sucrose-based admixture.** The waste-concrete mix had lower pumpability (1,400 versus 1,580 mm³/s); the authors attributed this to water absorption, irregular shape, roughness and porosity. \[6\]
**MEASURED — Aggregate size is constrained by extrusion hardware and print quality.** A review reports smaller aggregate sizes improved fresh-stage compressive performance; it also reports that 20 mm sand was attempted but print quality declined and void density increased as particle size rose. The Chinese association draft’s maximum coarse aggregate recommendation is ≤16 mm, conditional on the pump/nozzle system. \[4\] \[10\]
### 2.3 Strength and recycled content: reported range, not a design specification
**MEASURED — The published evidence is mixed rather than supporting one “safe” recycled-content ratio.** A 2025 review tabulates recycled-sand tests at 12.5–50% (0.9 mm) with slight compressive-strength reduction, 50–100% (3 mm) with decreased workability/increased porosity and lower strength at higher replacement, and fine recycled concrete/brick aggregate tests at 25–100% with reported ≥50 MPa compressive strength under particular mix conditions. Results are test/mix/equipment-specific. \[3\]
**MEASURED — The same review reports RFA-based printable mixtures at 25–60 MPa, and reports that 25–75% partial RFA replacement may have compressive strengths comparable to controls while 100% replacement causes notable reductions.** Across cited tests, strength reductions increase with replacement and vary by print direction: 9.4–29.2% (X), 12.5–33.1% (Y) and 14.9–40.0% (Z). This anisotropy matters for structural design. \[3\]
**MEASURED — Brick is not uniformly detrimental or beneficial.** One cited study reports a 390 mm-high printed façade element and satisfactory finish/accuracy after an optimised brick-aggregate approach; another reports that 64% brick aggregate cut cube compressive strength from 53.1 to 39.9 MPa (25%) and interlayer tensile strength from 1.72 to 1.37 MPa (20%). Both must be retained; neither validates an arbitrary mixed C&D feed. \[3\]
**MEASURED — In the ISARC laboratory study, 100% waste-concrete fine aggregate was printed only as a 200 × 100 × 100 mm slab and a 100 mm-diameter × 200 mm cylinder.** It printed a maximum 24 layers versus 28 for natural aggregate, and reported 7-day compressive strengths up to 5.9 MPa for the waste-concrete mix depending on load direction. These are laboratory specimens, not building walls or housing. \[6\]
**MEASURED — In the Polish laboratory study, the largest buildability object was a 150 mm centreline-diameter column, printed in 10 mm layers to a maximum 40 layers; its strength specimens were six printed layers cut to 40 × 40 × 160 mm.** It used clean, preselected ready-mix waste. This is a valuable material demonstration, but it is not a project-scale or occupied-building demonstration. \[5\]
**MODELED — The evidence supports a conservative pilot envelope, not a universal mix: assess processed concrete fines first, vary recycled replacement within a trial matrix, and reject any batch that fails nozzle continuity, buildability, directional strength, interlayer bond or durability tests.** A fixed “maximum percentage” for Dongjiakou is **UNKNOWN** until the precise printer, nozzle, binder, aggregate source and design load are specified. \[3\] \[5\] \[6\] \[10\]
### 2.4 Durability and environmental-performance caveat
**MEASURED — Recycled aggregates can raise water absorption and matrix discontinuity, while printed layer interfaces can be moisture/ion pathways.** The review recommends directional durability evidence such as freeze–thaw and chloride testing, water absorption, sorptivity, porosity and an anisotropy map—particularly at higher RFA/RCA substitutions. \[3\]
**MODELED — A 2021 peer-reviewed LCA/life-cycle-cost study of *modeled* recycled-concrete 3D-printed buildings found the 3D-printing route generally had higher environmental impact than conventional cast-in-situ concrete because printable concrete required more cement, even though more recycled aggregate reduced pollutant emissions.** It modeled economic advantages from avoiding formwork/labour, especially for irregular geometries; it was not a measured China project cost or an operational building result. \[13\]

### 3.2 The China project most often confused with C&D deployment
**REPORTED — The Wujiazhuang project record identifies a 106 m² one-storey shear-wall rural residence in Zhangjiakou, designed in 2020 and completed in 2021.** It describes three robotic printing platforms, on-site wall printing, separately printed arch roofs, a 40 mm print width and polypropylene-fibre concrete. \[7\]
**REPORTED — A 2023 media investigation states it was China’s first resident-occupied 3D-printed building, in Wujiazhuang, Hebei.** It says printing took 160 hours and cost about ¥200,000. \[8\]
**NOT CONFIRMED — Neither of these sources reports recycled C&D aggregate, recycled content by mass/volume, C&D source, grading, material test report or environmental product declaration.** It is incorrect to present the Wujiazhuang home as a validated C&D-waste-to-3D-printed-house case. \[7\] \[8\]
### 3.3 Vendor / interested-party claims
**REPORTED (vendor claim) — WinSun’s founder told *People’s Digest* that the printing ‘ink’ was made from processed construction waste and claimed a 50% construction-cost reduction.** The same article records independent architectural cautions that stiffness, strength and durability required further verification. This claim lacks mix proportion, contaminant data, building acceptance record and long-term performance data in the source. \[12\]
**REPORTED (vendor claim) — A 2023 interview with a WinSun manager said the company’s materials could incorporate various materials including recycled building stone.** The report also states that most domestic application was municipal works, highways, waterways and artistic installations; it does not document a C&D-aggregate housing project. \[8\]
## 4. Chinese standards and approval position
### 4.1 C&D management and recycled aggregate
**REPORTED — GB/T 25176-2010, *Recycled fine aggregate for concrete and mortar*, is a current recommended national standard.** The official National Standards Information Public Service Platform lists it as current, issued 26 September 2010 and effective 1 August 2011, under the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development. It regulates recycled fine aggregate for concrete/mortar; it is not a 3D-printing building approval. \[9\]
**REPORTED — JGJ/T 240-2011, *Technical Specification for Application of Recycled Aggregate*, is cited in peer-reviewed work as a China Architecture & Building Press technical specification.** The full authoritative text and current revision status were not retrieved from an official free database in this review; therefore its detailed limits should be checked directly with the project’s Chinese code consultant before any design reliance. \[17\]
**REPORTED — The State Council’s 2025 circular gives a policy pathway: construction/demolition/renovation waste should preferentially be used in recycled aggregate, recycled building materials and road materials; product certification and application standards are to be improved.** This does not establish that all recycled aggregate is suitable for structural 3D printing or that a building automatically complies. \[2\]
### 4.2 3D-printed concrete construction
**REPORTED — The *Technical Specification for 3D Printed Concrete Building Components* was publicly released by the China Building Materials Federation in 2024 as an association-standard consultation draft, with comments sought through 29 May 2024.** It was not an issued GB/GB/T or MOHURD national construction standard in the source reviewed. \[10\]
**REPORTED — That draft adopts a conditional material route for recycled fine aggregate and sets performance/quality provisions.** It requires incoming material inspection, defines printability/extrudability/buildability, gives C20–C50 strength-design ranges, requires testing and requires structural acceptance records. It does not declare that any C&D stream is automatically acceptable. \[10\]
**UNKNOWN / documented null — No issued Chinese national standard specifically governing whole-building concrete 3D printing was found in searches of the public National Standards platform and MOHURD-facing results.** The 2024 association draft is evidence of standardisation work, not final national code status. This conclusion is based on public-source search results and should be reconfirmed with a Chinese licensed design institute before investment or permitting.
### 4.3 What compliance would require at Dongjiakou
**MODELED — The minimum evidence package for an investable pilot would require: (1) a legally sourced, classified waste stream; (2) batch testing against applicable recycled-aggregate requirements; (3) printer-specific mix qualification; (4) directional mechanical, interlayer and durability testing; (5) a licensed structural design; (6) local construction/solid-waste approvals; and (7) quality-assurance records for each delivered batch.** This is an evidence-based application of the official classified-treatment requirements and the draft printing standard, not proof that approvals will be granted. \[2\] \[9\] \[10\]
## 5. Economics: China housing cost per square metre versus conventional construction
### 5.1 What was found
**REPORTED — A 2023 Chinese media investigation reported that the Wujiazhuang house had a 106 m² building area, took 160 hours to print and cost roughly ¥200,000, which the article reported as ¥2,000–2,500/m².** It explicitly said this was higher than the local rural self-built-house cost. The source does not provide a bill of quantities, a definition of whether land, foundation, services, roof, fit-out, machine depreciation, transport and overhead are included, or an equivalent conventional house scope. \[8\]
**REPORTED — A World Internet Conference/Guangming source likewise said the resident-occupied China house cost more than ¥200,000 whereas surveyed local brick-and-earth homes cost about ¥100,000.** The comparison is not normalised by floor area, specification, services, construction date or quality. It cannot yield a reliable conventional RMB/m² comparator. \[11\]
**REPORTED (vendor claim) — WinSun claimed a 50% cost reduction in 2014.** This comes from the founder in a press article. The same source contains expert cautions about unverified performance. It is not independently audited economics. \[12\]
**MODELED — Han et al. (2021) compared modeled recycled-concrete 3D-printed and cast-in-situ buildings and found economic advantages principally through avoiding formwork and labour, especially for irregular geometry.** The paper reports early-stage exploration and does not supply a verified completed-China-house price per m² in the accessible text. It therefore cannot validate the vendor or media figures. \[13\]

### 5.2 Economic conclusion
**UNKNOWN — A credible, like-for-like China comparison of fully completed 3D-printed housing cost per m² versus conventional construction cost per m² was not found.** It is particularly **UNKNOWN** for a coastal Qingdao/Dongjiakou site, for C&D-recycled printable material, and for a building that must meet Chinese structural/fire/durability requirements.

## 6. Direct answer for the Dongjiakou node
**MODELED — Decision assessment: C&D waste can be a technical source of *processed mineral feedstock* for 3D-printable mortar/concrete, but only for clean, segregated concrete/masonry fractions subjected to crushing, contamination removal, grading, moisture/absorption management and printer-specific qualification.** This is supported at laboratory scale. \[3\] \[5\] \[6\]
**UNKNOWN / documented null — C&D waste as a commercially proven feedstock for 3D-printed housing/buildings is not established.** No independently documented occupied building using recycled C&D aggregate was found; China’s real occupied printed-house evidence does not disclose recycled C&D aggregate. \[5\] \[6\] \[7\] \[8\]
**UNKNOWN — The annual Dongjiakou/Qingdao processable C&D quantity; exact composition; quality/yield after sorting; chloride/sulfate/organic contaminant content; optimum printable recycled-content ratio; structure/durability acceptance; local permit pathway; offtake; capital/operating cost; and RMB/m² economics.** None should be estimated from national tonnage or from vendor narratives.
**MODELED — A justified next evidence gate is a local, independently supervised sampling-and-trial campaign, not an immediate housing production claim.** It should compare sorted concrete and masonry streams against virgin aggregate in the actual printer/nozzle/mix system and include directional structural and durability tests before any commercial decision. \[2\] \[3\] \[10\]
