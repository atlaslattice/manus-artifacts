## Bottom-line answer for the Dongjiakou evidence register
**\[REPORTED\] Evidence verdict: PARTIALLY CONFIRMED.** Landfill excavation and remediation are real, and individual fractions—especially metals, cover-like soil, combustibles, or an ashfill metal concentrate—can be recovered. The proposition that legacy mixed landfill plastic can reliably supply a uniform recycled-content feedstock for blocks or 3D-printed construction is **not confirmed** by the evidence found. The credible commercial evidence is narrow and materially different from the proposed use: ash residues or relatively young waste, with value arising largely from airspace, remediation, land reuse, and energy disposal. Every project would require site-specific boreholes, composition testing, contamination testing, sorting trials, offtake specifications, a permitted destination for fines/inerts, and a fully measured carbon and cost baseline before a feedstock claim can be made.
> **Evidence-class convention.** **MEASURED** means a source reports field or laboratory measurement; **REPORTED** means an official, operator, trade, or descriptive account without independent audit in this review; **MODELED** means a scenario, LCA, or cost-benefit result; **UNKNOWN** means the reviewed evidence does not establish the item. These labels describe the strength of the evidence, not a claim of independent verification by this report.
## 1. What landfill mining is—and the evidence boundary
**\[REPORTED\]** Enhanced landfill mining (ELFM) excavates already buried waste, then separates, treats, recovers, re-landfills, or otherwise disposes of the outputs. It is distinct from sorting newly collected municipal waste. The European NEW-MINE programme produced concentrates and laboratory products, while its own summary says that competitiveness against primary materials remained unclear and economic incentives were needed. That is research and laboratory development, not commercial proof. \[5\]
**\[MEASURED\]** Excavated landfill material is commonly a heterogeneous mix of decomposed organics, soil-like fines, weathered minerals, glass/brick/concrete, metals, plastics, paper, textiles and combustibles. The fine fraction alone can comprise 40–80 wt.% of excavated waste depending on the size cut; the reviewed studies reported that it is often discarded because processing routes are unavailable or too expensive. \[11\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** There is no defensible generic recovery percentage for “a landfill,” because waste age, local waste composition, cover-soil practice, moisture, screening cut, contamination, historical industrial inputs, and permitted end uses differ. Applying one project’s mass split to Dongjiakou would be an unsupported estimate.

### Interpretation of the project record
**\[REPORTED\]** More than one actual excavation project exists, so the proposition “landfill mining has never been done” is false. But the phrase “commercial scale” needs care. The independently documented profitable case is a metals-rich **ashfill**, where the output was metal for recycling. The MSW projects found were limited pilots, a young-waste fuel project, remediation works, or a site-specific CBA. \[2\] \[3\] \[4\] \[6\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** A globally complete census of every operating landfill-mining project, with independently audited status, was not found. The table is therefore an evidence-based set of named cases, not a claim that no other operation exists.
**\[REPORTED\]** A Dutch project sometimes presented as landfill mining, Schoteroog near Haarlem, should *not* be counted as proof of legacy-waste material recovery. The former tip was re-opened to accept **new** waste; landfill fees funded containment/remediation and aftercare. The operator’s 1997 account reports 260,840 tonnes of new waste accepted from May 1996 to February 1997 and a modest operating result. It reports no excavation and sale of historic plastic. \[22\]
## 3. Recovery rates and condition of recovered material
### 3.1 Metals, fines, soil-like material, and combustibles
**\[MEASURED\]** At Mont-Saint-Guibert, recoverable-looking mass was not equivalent to a finished product. The largest output was \<4.5 mm fines, followed by inerts. Both need qualification before use; only small shares were ferrous and non-ferrous metals. \[4\]
**\[MEASURED\]** At Yingchun, the excavated material was overwhelmingly soil-like (75.02%), with a smaller combustible fraction (14.69%). These are composition measurements, not proof that the soil-like fraction met a soil standard, that combustibles met a fuel specification, or that either fraction was sold. \[6\]
**\[REPORTED\]** In the Florida cases, recovered “soil” was mainly reused as landfill cover rather than marketed as construction soil. The same source reports aged landfill recyclables were of questionable market quality, and the Naples demonstration found plastic and metal unacceptable to the resale market. \[3\]
**\[MEASURED\]** The technical reason is material condition. Fine fractions can include humic/decomposed organic matter, weathered mineral waste, fine metals, plastics and paper. Heavy-metal and leaching risks mean “soil-like” does not automatically mean clean soil or construction input. \[11\]
### 3.2 Aged plastic: why stable construction feedstock is a high bar
**\[MEASURED\]** A laboratory study of a polyethylene-rich fraction from an abandoned industrial landfill found mostly PE but also PP (\<5 wt.%), cellulose (\<4 wt.%), PS (\<2 wt.%), minerals, lignocellulosic fragments, metal particles, salt, and calcium/aluminium silicates. Thermogravimetry found 11% inorganic residual material. Washing had limited effectiveness for soil/dust particles. \[9\]
**\[MEASURED\]** The same unmodified landfill-recovered plastic had low tensile elongation at break (11 ± 2%) and brittle behaviour relative to LDPE; the authors attributed this principally to rigid inorganic and cellulose contaminants. The specific tested PE showed negligible thermo-oxidative degradation, so degradation is not the sole or necessarily dominant failure mode. This is an important counterpoint to a blanket assertion that every old plastic is chemically unusable. \[9\]
**\[MEASURED\]** More generally, polymer degradation can change mechanical, optical, or electrical properties and can include crazing, cracking, erosion, discoloration, phase separation, and delamination. Its rate depends on polymer, additives, physical form and landfill conditions; it cannot be predicted from landfill age alone. \[12\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Reviews identify mixed polymer types and grades, contamination by organic and inorganic matter, potential thermo-oxidative degradation, moisture, impurities and ash as core constraints. PVC/halogen-containing plastics also complicate thermal routes because halogens can damage equipment and contaminate products. \[9\] \[10\]
**\[MEASURED\]** There is favourable evidence only at laboratory/experimental scale: an integrated experimental/LCA study reported that a specifically processed ELFM plastic granulate had mechanical properties comparable with a primary material. It does **not** report a commercial specification, variability across landfill zones or batches, construction-code approval, or continuous commercial production. \[10\]
**\[UNKNOWN\] Plastic-specification verdict: NOT CONFIRMED.** A consistent recycled-content specification for landfill-mined mixed plastic cannot be assumed. A particular pre-characterised, PE-rich fraction may be compounded into a usable material after sorting, washing, milling and additives. That is materially different from accepting mixed excavation output into blocks or 3D-printing feedstock. **\[UNKNOWN\]** No field evidence found establishes product-to-product consistency, contaminant limits, rheology, long-term durability, or construction compliance for a landfill-mined plastic feedstock.
## 4. Economics: what was actually profitable, and per tonne of what?
**\[REPORTED\]** The most persuasive no-government-support profit claim concerns the ecomaine **ashfill**. Between December 2011 and March 2015 the operation recovered 34,352 Mg of metals with a conservative estimated value of USD 7.42m; mining also created 10,194 m³ airspace valued at USD 267,000. The reported extraction cost was USD 158 per Mg **of extracted metal**, not per Mg of all excavated waste. Therefore recovered value and cost **per tonne excavated are UNKNOWN**. \[2\]
**\[MODELED\]** The Yingchun CBA estimated average cost at USD 12.7 per tonne of stored waste, with excavation/hauling rental, processing and transport making up 88.2% of total cost. It estimated NPV of USD 1.92m–16.63m depending on scenario. The authors explicitly found sensitivity to land reuse, energy-recovery facilities and financial support through avoided post-closure care. This is a modelled case, not realised unaudited cash profit. \[6\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The Frey Farm project was said to be cash-flow neutral before airspace and cover-soil value, then USD 13.30/t positive after those internal benefits. The project used relatively young waste and a nearby combustor, so it is not transferable to an old-plastic product enterprise. \[3\]
**\[REPORTED\]** Naples cost about USD 115/t excavated, with award support covering much of cost. Perdido was justified by reclaimed airspace. These are public/operational value cases, not material-sales cases. \[3\]
**\[MODELED\]** The 2019 review assessed 15 LFM economic studies and concluded that they were mostly site-specific and that LFM had weak economic potential. It warned that comparisons mixed lab, pilot and process scales and that more than half lacked sensitivity analysis. \[1\]
**\[UNKNOWN\] Economic verdict: NOT CONFIRMED.** The evidence does **not** establish that a mixed legacy-MSW landfill-mining operation is generally profitable without subsidy, remediation obligation, landfill-airspace value, land redevelopment value, or other site-specific support. One special ashfill metals case has evidence of unsubsidised profitability. It must not be extrapolated to aged mixed plastic. **\[UNKNOWN\]** No audited unit economics were found for landfill-mined plastic sold as a consistent construction feedstock.
## 5. Energy, methane, and carbon balance
### What can be said
**\[MODELED\]** Laner et al. modelled net climate impacts of landfill mining from **–1,550 kg CO2e/Mg** (saving) to **+640 kg CO2e/Mg** (burden) of excavated waste. Nearly 90% of result variation was explained by landfill-gas management in the “leave in place” baseline, the background energy system, excavated-waste composition, and the waste-to-energy technology. This is a broad scenario range, not a measured energy or emissions result for a proposed facility. \[7\]
**\[MODELED\]** A China study based on pilot-scale demonstration projects found that an incineration-with-power pathway produced a climate impact of **134.10 kg CO2e/t aged refuse**. In its particular scenarios, combined mechanical recycling of high-quality plastic and chemical recycling of low-quality plastic reached **–15.46 kg CO2e/t aged refuse**. The authors state that industrial application of chemical recycling remains uncertain. \[8\]
**\[REPORTED\]** China’s 2012 national plan requires landfill-gas collection/use for sites where it has value, explicitly to reduce methane and other greenhouse-gas emissions. Excavation itself is regulated as an activity requiring leachate, methane, odour, dust and transport control; this indicates that excavation creates real release and safety hazards, rather than a frictionless methane credit. \[16\] \[18\]
### What cannot be claimed without a site model
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Measured kWh/t for excavation, screening, washing, drying, plastics sorting and compounding for a particular Dongjiakou legacy landfill was not found. No generic number should be imported from a shredder or from fresh plastic recycling.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Methane avoided per tonne excavated is not available without, at minimum, organic-carbon characterisation, waste age and moisture, local methane generation trajectory, cover oxidation, actual gas-collection efficiency, flaring/energy use, leakage, and a defined no-mining baseline.
**\[UNKNOWN\]** Carbon released from exposed fossil plastic, vehicle diesel, grid electricity, re-landfilling of rejects, thermal treatment, and transport cannot be netted against avoided virgin material without a destination-specific LCA.
**\[MODELED\] Carbon verdict: PARTIALLY CONFIRMED.** Excavation can reduce future landfill risks and may avoid some methane under a poorly controlled reference case, but it can also be a net climate burden. A carbon claim is credible only after transparent, site-specific LCA with a no-mining baseline and measured input data. The two peer-reviewed models disagree in sign across reasonable scenarios; they do not justify a universal emissions benefit. \[7\] \[8\]
## 6. Regulatory position
### European Union
**\[REPORTED\]** The EU Waste Framework Directive does not provide a special, automatic ELFM market status in the text reviewed. It requires a competent-authority permit for waste treatment, specifying waste types/quantities, technical requirements, safety, method, monitoring, and closure/aftercare. A recovered material ceases to be waste only if it has a specific use, market/demand, meets technical/product requirements, and poses no overall adverse environmental or human-health impact. \[13\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The EU Landfill Directive requires existing landfills to have conditioning plans and corrective measures where needed, with monitoring of landfill gas, leachate and groundwater during responsibility/aftercare. It does not itself confer a right to excavate or market an excavated fraction. Member-State permits and product, environmental and health rules remain material. \[14\]
**\[REPORTED\] EU position verdict: PARTIALLY CONFIRMED.** ELFM is possible under general waste, landfill, environmental-permitting and product rules, but an excavated “plastic” or “soil-like” fraction is waste until it satisfies the applicable recovery/end-of-waste conditions. **\[UNKNOWN\]** A pan-EU ELFM-specific authorisation regime or EU-wide end-of-waste specification for mixed aged landfill plastic was not found in the primary legal texts reviewed.
### China
**\[REPORTED\]** China has had a China-specific legacy-waste programme since at least 2012. A joint Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development/NDRC/Ministry of Environmental Protection notice required provinces to survey and register informal dumps and substandard facilities, including location, area, tonnage, composition, degree of ageing, landfill years, pollution-control status, surroundings and risk. High-risk sites could seek central budget investment after plans were completed. \[15\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The State Council’s Twelfth Five-Year municipal-waste plan forecast 1,882 stock-waste governance projects: 503 upgrades of substandard facilities, 802 sanitary-landfill closures, and 577 informal-dump projects. It says excavation/resource use may be used at large, valuable or land-constrained sites, including metals recovery, high-calorific waste to incineration, large inorganic material to backfill, and—only where suitable—screened fines as landscaping soil. It does not declare all recovered fractions safe or marketable. \[16\]
**\[REPORTED\]** A 2017 four-ministry programme required a nationwide survey of informal dumps, including life waste, C&D waste, industrial waste, hazardous waste, agricultural waste, and certain floating waste. \[17\]
**\[REPORTED\]** The Ministry of Ecology and Environment issued a **draft for consultation** in 2025 covering excavation of municipal solid-waste landfills. It applies to land redevelopment and off-site pollution remediation, requires investigation, methane testing/control, leachate management, enclosed/odour-controlled sorting, and testing before use of outputs. The draft says light oversize should normally go to incineration/cement kiln, can be recycled or made into RDF; inert fractions require relevant construction-material compliance; fines used as planted soil or construction soil must meet the specified standards. As a consultation draft, it is not evidence of a final binding national ELFM product standard. \[18\]
**\[REPORTED\] China position verdict: CONFIRMED for remediation programme; NOT CONFIRMED for proven plastic feedstock.** China has formal legacy-waste remediation, survey and public-investment pathways and named excavation projects. The evidence found supports controlled remediation and destination testing—not a presumption that legacy mixed plastic is a standardised recycled product.
## 7. China: named examples and evidence quality
**\[REPORTED\]** **Yingchun landfill, Jingmen, Hubei** is the clearest published China case found. The project had started in 2013; the published paper used field data and modelled several economics scenarios. It is evidence of project activity and composition testing, not audited commercial recovery performance. \[6\]
**\[REPORTED\]** **Tangxia landfill, Zengcheng, Guangzhou** received a 2023 EIA approval for 900 t/day of stock-waste excavation, screening, processing and resource use over five years. The approval requires stabilisation, landfill-gas extraction and flaring, leachate treatment, odour/dust controls, and backfilling of unusable output. Its capacity is a permitted design value, not measured operating throughput or recovery. \[19\]
**\[REPORTED\]** **Yulong landfill, Luohu, Shenzhen** began a full-excavation relocation/remediation project in 2024. The official announcement states \>2.5m m³ waste, CNY 2.17bn total investment, odour control and third-party monitoring. It demonstrates public remediation at scale, but no product-yield, sales, energy, or profitability data. \[20\]
**\[MEASURED\]** An unnamed informal landfill in a rainy southern-China case study occupied roughly 28,000 m², held about 240,000 m³ waste/mudstone and 30,000 m³ leachate, and had water up to 12 m deep. The intervention was drainage, leachate treatment, soil replacement and immobilisation—not extraction to plastic feedstock. \[21\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** The sources reviewed do not establish how many Chinese legacy-landfill remediation projects have completed, their national aggregate throughput, the proportion of outputs sold as materials, or audited returns. The programme is real; the commercial resource-recovery performance record is thin.
## 8. Implications for a construction / 3D-printing feedstock claim
**\[NOT CONFIRMED\]** Legacy landfill material should not enter a construction product claim as “Trashium” or any equivalent uniform feedstock merely because it has passed a screen or shredder. Screened outputs are mixtures. The Chinese draft guide itself differentiates light oversize, inert material and fines and requires testing/destination control. \[18\]
**\[UNKNOWN\]** For Dongjiakou, no evidence establishes: the legacy landfill’s plastic fraction; polymer mix; chlorine/PVC; moisture; ash; particle-size distribution; heavy metals/organic contaminants; polymer molecular weight; melt-flow/rheology; odour; batch variability; required washing/drying energy; product recipe; structural performance; fire performance; leaching; Chinese building-code acceptance; or willingness of a block/3D-printing customer to accept the material.
**\[REPORTED\]** The defensible near-term framing is therefore **remediation with selective recovery**, not a guaranteed plastics-to-construction value chain. Candidate destinations may include metal recycling, carefully qualified fuel/thermal treatment, landfill cover/backfill, permitted inerts processing, and—only after extensive qualification—specific polymer-rich plastic streams. \[3\] \[11\] \[18\]

## NULL RESULTS
The following targeted searches were conducted. The listed evidence was **not found**; this is not proof that it does not exist.
- **Commercial mixed-MSW plastic recovery:** Searches for “enhanced landfill mining commercial projects recovery rates economics,” “landfill mining projects recovery per tonne excavated profitability,” and named European/North American projects found pilots, remediation, fuel/cover/airspace cases, and one ashfill-metals profit case—but no independently audited commercial mixed-aged-plastic-to-construction operation.
- **Per-tonne excavation mass balances:** Searches for recovery rates at ecomaine, Yingchun, Mont-Saint-Guibert, Frey Farm, Perdido and Naples found selected yields or output totals, but no complete comparable mass balance for most sites.
- **Unsubsidised economics:** No audited, generalisable profit-and-loss account was found for mixed legacy-MSW landfill mining based principally on material sales. The ecomaine ashfill case is the exception, but it is not mixed MSW/plastic. \[2\]
- **Construction / 3D-printing feedstock:** No peer-reviewed or official source was found demonstrating a commercial consistent-specification landfill-mined mixed-plastic feedstock for construction blocks or 3D printing.
- **Energy intensity:** No measured, generalisable kWh/t value was found for the full aged-waste excavation, sorting, washing, drying and plastic-compounding chain. Published climate results were LCA/scenario models, not a universal electricity measurement. \[7\] \[8\]
- **EU ELFM-specific product regime:** No EU-wide ELFM-specific permit or end-of-waste specification for aged mixed landfill plastic was found in the Waste Framework Directive and Landfill Directive texts reviewed. \[13\] \[14\]
- **China commercial outcome data:** No national official database was found showing realised recovery yields, plastic product quality, revenues or profits from China’s informal-landfill/legacy-waste programme.
