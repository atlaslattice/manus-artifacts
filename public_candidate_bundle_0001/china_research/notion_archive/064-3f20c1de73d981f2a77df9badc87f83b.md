## Executive summary
- **MEASURED —** Group arts interventions for adults aged over 55 without dementia reduced depression by **d=0.70** in a meta-analysis of controlled studies; publication-bias adjustment reduced the estimate to **d=0.42**. This is credible health evidence for a defined population, not proof that building cultural infrastructure will produce the same result. \[S03\]
- **REPORTED —** “Arts on prescription” programmes showed a **5.82-point** mean pre/post WEMWBS wellbeing increase, but every meta-analysed study was a medium-quality pre/post design without a comparison group. The popular causal claim is stronger than this evidence permits. \[S02\]
- **MEASURED —** China’s culture and related industries produced **CNY 5.9464 trillion** of value added, **4.59% of GDP**, in 2023. This establishes sector scale, not that a Dongjiakou arts facility or restored building will deliver a local return. \[S08\]
- **REPORTED —** Qingdao’s Dabao Island historic district reported high post-restoration footfall and commercial occupancy, but no accessible source supplied programme cost, incremental revenue, local jobs, resident wellbeing, displacement, or a counterfactual. It is a documented activation case, **not** a documented net-return case. \[S11\]
- **MODELED —** A Qingdao li-yuan refurbishment case simulated a **11.8–12.3%** reduction in peak heating demand under two retrofit models. It did not compare lifecycle cost with demolition/replacement, nor test traditional craft restoration. \[S17\]
- **REPORTED —** Heritage tourism can bring livelihoods, skills and cultural continuity, but a systematic review also found displacement, resource pressure, cultural dilution and benefits bypassing residents. Tourism-led restoration is therefore **MIXED EVIDENCE**, not automatically a QOL intervention. \[S10\]
- **REPORTED —** Museums and libraries are associated with several social-wellbeing dimensions, and targeted museum programmes can improve wellbeing for low-wellbeing participants. The strongest facility evidence concerns staffed participation and social connection, not a building’s existence alone. \[S14\] \[S15\]
- **REPORTED —** China’s current heritage framework requires protection first, minimal intervention, and control of large-scale relocation and overdevelopment in historic districts. It permits compatible use but does not demonstrate economic payback. \[S12\] \[S13\]
## What this evidence supports — and does not
**SUPPORTED, with conditions —** Participatory arts can be treated as a plausible QOL service for workers, residents and older people if it is a recurring, accessible programme with measured participation and outcomes. The best quantified health result in this register is for **group-based interventions**, rather than passive cultural consumption or construction. \[S03\]
**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED —** Conservation and adaptive reuse can preserve cultural value, support local activity, and in one Qingdao model improve thermal performance. However, no source found here supports a general claim that restoring older Chinese fabric using traditional materials is financially cheaper than replacement, or that it will generate a positive financial return at Dongjiakou. Costs, condition, safety, operating model, heritage significance, energy performance and displacement risk must be established building by building. \[S12\] \[S17\] \[S18\]
**NOT SUPPORTED as stated —** “A museum, public artwork, performance venue, or attractive heritage district automatically improves mental health, social cohesion, local GDP or property values.” Existing evidence is often observational, programme-specific, self-reported, or based on visitor/activity proxies. The effect of construction alone is **UNKNOWN**. \[S14\] \[S16\]
**UNKNOWN —** No outcome evaluation was located for arts, heritage restoration, traditional-building restoration, public art, library/museum provision, or cultural QOL in Dongjiakou itself. No applicable evidence was found on effects on flora, fauna or AI quality of life; those are not established outcomes in the literature reviewed for this item.
## 1. Arts participation, mental health, wellbeing, cognition and social cohesion
### Mental health and wellbeing
**MEASURED — controlled-study synthesis.** Quinn, Millard and Jones identified 50 controlled studies of group arts interventions for adults aged over 55 without dementia; 39 were included in meta-analysis. The depression analysis covered 36 studies and 3,360 participants: pooled **Cohen’s d=0.70** (95% CI 0.54–0.87; p\<0.001). The anxiety analysis covered 10 studies and 949 participants: **d=0.76** (95% CI 0.37–1.52; p\<0.001). The review detected publication bias for depression; trim-and-fill produced **d=0.42** (95% CI 0.35–0.50). This is the most robust quantitative mental-health signal identified, while still restricted by population, intervention variation and publication bias. It is not medical advice and does not establish that arts replace clinical care. \[S03\]
**REPORTED — uncontrolled programme synthesis.** Jensen and colleagues’ systematic review/meta-analysis of Arts on Prescription found that seven studies using the WEMWBS wellbeing scale had a pooled mean pre/post improvement of **5.82 points** (95% CI 4.90–6.75). The same paper reports moderate heterogeneity (**I²=57%**) and, crucially, that all studies entering the meta-analysis were medium-quality pre/post designs with no comparison group. Referral, regression to the mean, concurrent support, expectation effects and selective retention can therefore explain part or all of the measured change. Calling this a confirmed treatment effect would be inaccurate. \[S02\]
**REPORTED — broad mapping, not a causal effect estimate.** The WHO scoping review located more than 3,000 studies across prevention, health promotion and treatment/management. It included uncontrolled pilots, case studies and cross-sectional surveys as well as longitudinal cohorts and randomised trials. Its breadth is valuable for identifying mechanisms and settings, but it did not pool effects or apply a uniform quality threshold. The statement that arts have a “major role” is an evidence-map conclusion, not a single quantified causal estimate. \[S01\]
**MODELED and MEASURED — evidence synthesis with selection warning.** OECD’s review/meta-analysis concludes that causal evidence exists across several cultural activities, but also reports major evidence gaps: active arts engagement/writing and performing arts/cinema had limited medical evidence, and observed participation statistics are affected by strong preference-based selection. Its time-use analysis covered Canada, France, Italy, the United Kingdom and the United States. This supports measuring both participation and outcomes rather than inferring benefit from ticket sales or facility availability. \[S05\]
### Cognition
**REPORTED — mixed-method systematic review.** McQuade and O’Sullivan reviewed 93 studies (2013–2020) of arts and creativity in later life. They describe evidence on regular music/singing and improved cognitive function as “promising,” not conclusive; visual/creative arts for loneliness, community and social connectedness as “preliminary”; and theatre/drama evidence as requiring more research. The review did not provide a pooled cognitive effect size. Claims that any arts programme prevents cognitive decline are therefore **UNKNOWN** on the evidence gathered here. \[S04\]
**MEASURED — limited scope.** The controlled group-arts meta-analysis excluded people with dementia, so it cannot be used to assert a dementia-treatment or prevention effect. \[S03\]
### Social cohesion
**REPORTED — targeted programme evidence.** A rapid review of place-based arts and culture concluded that targeted museum interventions with a volunteering or social-prescribing component improved subjective and mental wellbeing for people with low wellbeing, especially when rooted in local culture/heritage and connecting people. It found only some **temporary** happiness/life-satisfaction effects from increased participation through mega-events, alongside increases in bridging social capital. This supports active, relational programming more than one-off spectacle. Rapid review methods and heterogeneous underlying studies lower certainty. \[S15\]
**REPORTED — observational association.** The IMLS national exploratory study used county-level multivariate models and 24 case studies; it reported associations between museum/library presence and use and ten social-wellbeing dimensions after accounting for economic standing and racial/ethnic composition. Associations do not rule out reverse causation or residual confounding: places with stronger civic capacity may both support facilities and have higher wellbeing. The study is also United States-specific. \[S14\]
**UNKNOWN —** No controlled or quasi-experimental estimate was located that isolates the social-cohesion effect of opening a general cultural facility in a Chinese industrial-port locality, or that establishes a durable effect on conflict, trust or worker–resident relations at Dongjiakou.
## 2. Cultural and creative industries: economic scale versus local causal impact
**MEASURED — global sector/trade scale.** UNESCO reported that creative and cultural sectors accounted for **6.2% of all workers** and **3.1% of global GDP** in its 2022 report, while noting 10 million culture/creativity jobs lost globally in 2020. These are aggregate statistics, not return-on-investment estimates for cultural facilities. \[S07\]
**MEASURED — trade data.** UNCTAD reports creative-services exports of **US\$1.4 trillion** in 2022, up 29% from 2017, and creative-goods exports of **US\$713 billion**, up 19%. Its country survey found creative-economy contributions ranging from **0.5% to 7.3% of GDP** and **0.5% to 12.5% of employment**. The same source says cultural, recreational and heritage services represented only **0.6%** of creative-services exports in 2022; software and R&D dominate. A claim that a heritage district will create an export-scale “creative economy” would be marketing without a market study. \[S06\]
**MEASURED — China national accounts.** China’s National Bureau of Statistics puts 2023 culture-and-related-industry value added at **CNY5.9464 trillion**, 4.59% of GDP. Culture-related services were CNY4.1158 trillion (69.2%); core cultural areas were CNY4.2312 trillion (71.2%). The NBS classification also includes culture-related manufacturing, wholesale/retail, equipment and consumption-terminal production. It should not be presented as the output of arts participation, heritage restoration or traditional practices alone. \[S08\]
**UNKNOWN —** No Dongjiakou-level baseline was found for cultural employment, creative-business turnover, cultural spending leakage, tourism overnight stays, local supplier capture, or willingness to pay. Without those data, a local multiplier, job forecast or cost saving would be an estimate and is not supplied here.
## 3. Heritage restoration economics and Chinese built fabric
### Cases with observed post-intervention activity
**REPORTED — Qingdao district activity, not a return calculation.** Dazhong Online reported that Dabao Island’s historic-district protection/update began in 2019 and that about **80,000 m²** of protected buildings were repaired in 2022–23. The core has 183 li-yuan buildings and covers 670,000 m². It reported 346,000 visits in the 2023 May Day period, 3.63 million during July–August beer-festival months, and 620,000 in the October period. Around 100 brands had opened; reported occupancy was 75% in the south area and 55% in the north, with a 70% signed rate. These are project/local-media operational statistics and may be influenced by festival programming, city-wide tourism and commercial leasing; they lack cost, pre-period comparator, net revenue, quality jobs, resident outcomes and displacement data. The verifiable conclusion is activity/occupancy, not positive net economic return. \[S11\]
**MODELED — historical-district regeneration proxy outcomes.** Zhao et al. applied difference-in-differences models to two historical districts using multi-source data and an authors’ composite regeneration/spatial-behaviour metric. Reported controlled treatment effects were 2.6% and 2.2% for two distinct models. The authors also found population density, functional scale and transport accessibility mattered. This is stronger than unadjusted footfall, but the outcome is not audited revenue, jobs, resident wellbeing or an investment payback. It demonstrates change in a proxy of spatial vitality, not a general heritage-restoration ROI. \[S09\]
**MODELED — Qingdao retrofit energy case.** Huang et al. simulated two refurbishment models for an early twentieth-century Qingdao li-yuan building. Relative to the baseline, peak heating demand fell to 24,700 kWh (11.8% reduction) and 24,550 kWh (approximately 12.3% reduction); cooling demand slightly increased after insulation. This is an energy-model result in one building, not a measured energy bill, lifecycle carbon assessment, financial appraisal, traditional-material test, or demolition-versus-rebuild comparison. \[S17\]
### Cases and evidence where benefits did not reach communities
**REPORTED — systematic-review synthesis of adverse outcomes.** Brooks et al. found studies reporting forced displacement, environmental degradation and resource overuse, barriers to tourism employment, dependence on tourism income, loss/dilution of cultural practices, instability, crime/disease risks and lack of direct benefit to local communities. Benefits—such as skills, employment, services, livelihoods and preservation—also appeared. The review explicitly calls for more longitudinal and qualitative work. This is evidence that heritage tourism can fail its host-community QOL test, but it does **not** quantify how often this occurs or prove the same outcome at Dongjiakou. \[S10\]
**UNKNOWN — documented failed-return case.** Within the time and accessible sources for this register, no credible, audited Chinese case was located that compares restoration cost with a valid counterfactual and demonstrates a negative net financial return. This is a useful null finding: anecdotal claims of either universal payback or universal loss should not be treated as evidence.
### Traditional Chinese building crafts, materials, repair or replacement
**REPORTED — heritage/craft status.** China’s official intangible-heritage platform describes Chinese traditional timber-building techniques as a representative UNESCO intangible cultural heritage element, inscribed in 2009, and identifies trained craftspeople, organisations and multiple regional traditions. This validates the cultural-heritage value and the existence of a safeguarding ecosystem; it does not demonstrate a cost advantage over replacement. \[S18\]
**REPORTED — policy requires conservation discipline.** The revised Cultural Relics Protection Law took effect on 1 March 2025. National Cultural Heritage Administration guidance states that repair, use and relocation of immovable relics must retain the original state and apply minimal intervention. It also says historic district/town/village tourism development must implement protection measures, control large-scale relocation, prevent overdevelopment and strengthen whole-area protection and living transmission. This makes indiscriminate replacement legally and culturally problematic for protected assets, but does not determine the appropriate intervention for unlisted buildings. \[S12\]
**REPORTED — national plan.** The 14th Five-Year Plan for cultural-relic protection calls for repair of major timber heritage buildings, support for traditional-material production and construction-skill transmission in architectural conservation, and technology work on wood-structure diagnosis, disease prevention and evaluation. It is a policy/programme commitment, not evidence that traditional materials outperform contemporary materials economically or technically in every coastal setting. \[S13\]
**UNKNOWN — cost-effectiveness versus replacement.** No peer-reviewed, China-specific, head-to-head lifecycle-cost study was found that compares: (1) diagnosis-led conservation using traditional Chinese craft/materials, (2) compatible modern repair, and (3) demolition/replacement, while holding function, safety, lifespan and heritage value constant. No reliable general percentage saving should be stated. A project appraisal needs building-by-building condition surveys, structural/fire/coastal-corrosion assessments, quantity/cost plans, maintenance schedules, operational-energy model, heritage-significance assessment and explicit social/displacement account.
## 4. Museums, libraries and performance/cultural venues
**REPORTED — programme rather than building effect.** The What Works Wellbeing rapid review finds that museum interventions with volunteering or social prescribing can improve subjective and mental wellbeing among people with low wellbeing, especially where participants make social connections. It does not establish that opening a museum, library or theatre without inclusive programming achieves the same outcome. \[S15\]
**REPORTED — facility associations.** The IMLS study’s multivariate county analysis and 24 case studies support the proposition that libraries and museums can act as civic connectors. The design measures association rather than an intervention effect, and it is not an economic ROI study. This evidence is best interpreted as support for a staffed, accessible institution that builds partnerships, not for a landmark building as a standalone cure for isolation. \[S14\]
**REPORTED — Chinese township service evidence.** A study in Xuling Town, Anhui used interviews, a survey and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis. It found high satisfaction configurations associated with cultural facilities, activity quality, cultural talent, management and traditional festival culture; its authors caution that the sample was medium sized, context-specific and not publicly available. The study supports measuring service quality and local practices, but it cannot establish causality or be generalised to Dongjiakou. \[S20\]
**UNKNOWN — performance venues.** No systematic review or credible Chinese quasi-experimental evaluation was located that gives a general effect size for constructing a performance venue on community mental health, cohesion, employment or local spending. Venue claims without attendance, participation, inclusion and outcome data are marketing.
## 5. Public art and beautification
**REPORTED — systematic-review mapping, weak causal base.** Cheung et al. searched 839 articles and selected 50 for a systematic review of public-art impacts. The literature describes potential effects in placemaking, society, culture, economy, sustainability, wellbeing, wisdom and innovation. The authors state that primary systematic research on how communities perceive, experience and engage with public art remains lacking; they describe claims as liable to criticism as “overblown and unrealistic” and note that economy, wellbeing, wisdom and innovation were under-studied. Thus, public art is a plausible mechanism for place identity and engagement, but its average QOL or economic effect is **UNKNOWN**. \[S16\]
**REPORTED / marketing distinction.** Visitor increases around particular installations or claims that art “adds value” are not, by themselves, proof of resident wellbeing, local income retention, social cohesion or causal effect. For Dongjiakou, a public-art proposal should be classified as **plausible mechanism with no local outcome evidence** until independently measured. \[S16\]
**UNKNOWN —** No Dongjiakou-specific public-art baseline, safety perception, vandalism rate, use count, resident preference study, or before/after wellbeing evaluation was located.
## 6. Chinese policy and relevant local programmes
**REPORTED — national policy framework.** China’s governing framework includes the 2024 revised Cultural Relics Protection Law (effective 2025), the 14th Five-Year Plan for Cultural Relics Protection and Technological Innovation, and national safeguarding of traditional building techniques. The law and plan emphasise protection first, rational use, minimal intervention, original-state integrity, prevention of overdevelopment and integration with public culture. They create duties and permissions, rather than verified benefits. \[S12\] \[S13\] \[S18\]
**REPORTED — Qingdao relevance.** Dabao Island is a Qingdao historic-district programme with documented repairs, event activity and commercial occupation. It is geographically relevant as a municipal comparator, but it is not in Dongjiakou and its source does not identify causal economic, wellbeing or anti-displacement results. \[S11\]
**UNKNOWN — Dongjiakou programme.** Searches in Chinese for “董家口 历史文化 保护 修缮 文物 文化遗产”, “黄岛区 传统村落 修缮 文化遗产 经济效益”, and “胶南 董家口 文化遗产 保护 修复” did not locate a documented Dongjiakou heritage-restoration programme with outcome evaluation. Nearby/administrative-area heritage assets should not be assumed to be in the industrial node without a local cultural-relic inventory and authority verification.
## 7. A verifiable cultural-QOL measurement register
**REPORTED — framework.** UNESCO’s Culture\|2030 Indicators provides 22 thematic indicators across four dimensions and is designed for reliable culture data from statistical institutes, ministries, observatories, surveys and other sources. It is a measurement framework, not proof that culture improves outcomes. \[S19\]
For Dongjiakou, track a public baseline before intervention and repeat at 6, 12, 24 and 36 months. Pre-specify targets, calculation rules, sampling and an independent evaluator. Compare with a similar untreated locality where possible, and report negative as well as positive change. Do not turn proxy activity into outcome claims.

## Decision rule for this node
**MIXED EVIDENCE.** Arts participation has meaningful controlled-study evidence for mental-health symptoms in a specific older-adult population and suggestive, weaker evidence for wider wellbeing, cognition and cohesion. \[S03\] \[S04\] \[S15\] **MEASURED —** Cultural industries are economically large, but national/global aggregates do not imply a local intervention return. \[S06\] \[S08\] **REPORTED and MODELED —** Heritage restoration and cultural facilities can activate places and support participation, but local net benefit depends on governance, affordability, resident control, recurring programming, building condition and cost. \[S09\] \[S10\] \[S11\] \[S14\] Tourism/beautification claims are particularly vulnerable to conflating footfall with wellbeing and gross activity with net benefit. \[S11\] \[S16\]
**REPORTED — policy and evidence-led condition.** A defensible Dongjiakou programme is conditional: protect and document significant fabric; use skilled traditional craft where significance and technical assessment justify it; provide low-barrier, recurring participatory culture; require anti-displacement protections; and publish the register above. \[S03\] \[S10\] \[S12\] \[S13\] Promotion from UNKNOWN to a pilot is reasonable, but promotion to “net-positive” requires measured benefits and harms, not branding.

## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN
- **UNKNOWN — Dongjiakou baseline.** Cultural assets, legal designation, condition, ownership, craft availability, local cultural participation, library/museum/service provision, arts access, heritage knowledge and resident preferences at the node.
- **UNKNOWN — local economics.** Capital and whole-life repair costs; cost and carbon of replacement; local visitor spend; local supplier capture; tenant turnover; local jobs/wages; subsidy; local multiplier; and distribution of gains/losses.
- **UNKNOWN — traditional-craft comparison.** China-specific, comparable lifecycle-cost, safety, maintenance, durability and carbon evidence for traditional repair versus compatible modern repair versus replacement in a saline/coastal-industrial climate.
- **UNKNOWN — health and cognition transfer.** Whether arts effects found elsewhere transfer to Dongjiakou workers, residents, younger people, shift workers or people with clinical conditions; no medical recommendation follows from the reviews.
- **UNKNOWN — permanent facility effect.** Independent effect of a museum, library, public-art installation or performance venue absent sustained, equitable participation programmes.
- **UNKNOWN — equity and harm.** Resident displacement, rent escalation, access by lower-income/migrant/shift-working residents, cultural appropriation, noise/crowding, public-space exclusion and who controls tourism revenue.
- **UNKNOWN — ecological/AI outcomes.** Direct effects of arts/heritage interventions on flora, fauna, AI welfare or broader industrial-node ecology.
## NULL RESULTS
- **NULL RESULT —** Chinese searches for a Dongjiakou-specific heritage restoration programme with independently measured economic, tourism, wellbeing, or social-cohesion outcomes did not locate one. Searches: “董家口 历史文化 保护 修缮 文物 文化遗产”; “黄岛区 传统村落 修缮 文化遗产 经济效益”; “胶南 董家口 文化遗产 保护 修复”.
- **NULL RESULT —** No credible audited Chinese heritage-restoration case was found that presents full restoration cost, counterfactual/replacement cost, net revenue, local-job quality, displacement and resident-QOL outcome in one evaluation.
- **NULL RESULT —** No China-specific head-to-head lifecycle-cost study was found for traditional Chinese building craft/material restoration versus modern compatible repair versus demolition/replacement under comparable safety/function conditions.
- **NULL RESULT —** No meta-analysis was found that gives a pooled general cognitive effect size for community arts participation in an all-adult population; the older-adult review describes cognition evidence as promising and heterogeneous. \[S04\]
- **NULL RESULT —** No robust systematic-review effect size was found for building a performance venue, public artwork, museum or library on community-wide mental health, cohesion, local GDP or property value. The public-art review itself identifies this evaluation gap. \[S16\]
- **NULL RESULT —** No evidence was found that cultural restoration produces verified net-positive outcomes for flora, fauna or AI welfare at industrial nodes.
