## Executive summary
- **SUPPORTED — MEASURED:** Regular physical activity has a strong, curvilinear association with lower all-cause mortality, coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes and incident depression. The largest marginal association is between doing none and doing some; effects above the guideline dose diminish. These are predominantly prospective observational findings, so they establish a robust association rather than a facility-caused health outcome. \[1\] \[2\] \[3\] \[4\] \[5\] \[6\]
- **SUPPORTED FOR INDIVIDUALS; NOT YET ESTABLISHED FOR A NEW NODE — MEASURED:** In controlled trials among adults aged over 50, exercise improved cognitive function by a small-to-moderate standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.29. This is stronger causal evidence than the mortality associations, but it is not a measured effect of building a sports facility or of preventing dementia at Dongjiakou. \[7\]
- **MIXED EVIDENCE — MEASURED:** Walkable, park-rich and transit-accessible neighbourhoods are associated with substantially more accelerometer-measured activity, and some route and park interventions increase use or activity. Infrastructure alone often does **not** raise population-wide activity: 41.7% of walking/cycling built-environment changes in an umbrella review were null, and the better community-wide trials in a Cochrane review did not increase population activity. \[10\] \[12\] \[13\] \[15\]
- **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — MEASURED:** Sustained behavioural programmes produce small objective gains: SMD 0.18 for moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) at 24 months, equivalent to a modelled 21–25 minutes/week in that review. This is a different intervention from infrastructure and not a fair basis for claiming that a park alone will produce the same gain. \[14\]
- **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — MEASURED:** Workplace physical-activity programmes show modest, heterogeneous improvements in work attendance (standardized effect 0.19 in a meta-analysis). A broader workplace-intervention review reported 2.65 fewer sickness-absence days in moderate-quality trials, but heterogeneity was extreme (I²=98%) and programmes were not activity-only. A reliable pooled effect on objective output/productivity was not available. \[8\] \[9\]
- **REPORTED:** China’s 2021–25 National Fitness Plan targets 38.5% regular participation and universal community 15-minute fitness circles; official progress reporting gives 37.2% regular participation in September 2024 and 3.0 m² sports-ground area per person at end-2024. These are provision/participation indicators, not a causal evaluation of health gains from the programme. \[17\] \[18\]
- **UNKNOWN:** A defensible capital or lifecycle **cost per capita** for sports parks, fitness centres, trails, pitches, community gyms or spectator venues at Dongjiakou cannot be supplied from the evidence found. National figures mix actual cost, subsidy ceilings and broad programme totals; they cannot be converted to a per-capita cost without unsupported assumptions. \[20\] \[21\] \[22\]
- **MIXED EVIDENCE:** Active sport participation is associated with better mental-health and social outcomes, but the adult sport-specific literature is mostly observational and low quality. Spectator sport has plausible social-identity and social-contact mechanisms, yet no verified population-health effect size adequate for an investment case. \[23\] \[24\]
## Register conventions
**MEASURED** means a study directly measured an exposure and outcome. It does **not** automatically mean the effect is causal: prospective cohort and cross-sectional findings remain vulnerable to confounding, selection and reverse causation. **REPORTED** means an administrative, policy or programme source reported the figure; it is not independently verified here. **MODELED** means a quantity was calculated from assumptions rather than directly observed. **UNKNOWN** means no usable number or outcome evidence was found; it is not a zero effect.
The distinction matters for the Dongjiakou decision: a health effect of *being active* is not evidence that providing a facility will cause a node-wide health effect. No clinical or personal exercise advice is given here.
## 1. Health dose–response: what is measured, and what is not
**MEASURED (prospective cohort evidence):** WHO’s adult public-health recommendation is 150–300 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days. WHO characterises the aerobic dose-response for mortality, cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality and incident diabetes as curvilinear with moderate-certainty evidence: some activity is better than none, and relative benefits diminish as volume rises. The recommendation is a population-health guideline, not proof that any particular facility causes people to reach the dose. \[1\]

## 2. Productivity and sickness absence: real signal, weak investment precision
**MEASURED:** A meta-analysis of workplace physical-activity interventions across about 38,231 participants found standardized effects of **0.21** for physical-activity behaviour, **0.57** for fitness and **0.19** (95% CI 0.11–0.27) for work attendance in two-group post-test comparisons. The authors judged effects variable and noted that activity was rarely measured objectively. The attendance result is evidence of a modest average difference in a heterogeneous research set; it is not a yuan-per-worker productivity saving. \[8\]
**MEASURED:** In the same review, job-stress effect size was 0.33 in three post-test comparisons, with a confidence interval crossing zero (−0.06 to 0.73), and job satisfaction was 0.20 (0.06–0.35) in five pre–post comparisons. These are work-related outcomes, not verified output per hour or facility-return measures. \[8\]
**MEASURED:** A later review of 47 workplace RCTs pooled the five moderate-quality studies reporting absence (2,195 participants) and found **−2.65 sickness-absence days** (95% CI −4.49 to −0.81). The I² statistic was **98%**, so the size varied markedly across studies. The review covers counselling, education, multicomponent and physical-activity interventions, rather than isolated sport facilities or exercise alone. \[9\]
**MEASURED:** That later review could not pool productivity/presenteeism because too few studies used comparable measures and instruments varied; it explicitly warns that many included studies were weak quality and that outcome tools often were not validated or did not measure the claimed construct directly. Isolated positive pilot findings therefore do not establish a general productivity return. \[9\]
**Verdict on productivity:** **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** Improving activity and fitness at work is a reasonable candidate health intervention, and modest absence/work-attendance effects have been measured. The claim that sports infrastructure will reliably improve industrial productivity or produce a known financial payback is **NOT SUPPORTED** by this evidence. A Dongjiakou business case requires baseline register-based absence, job-role/shift data, intervention uptake, comparator sites and pre-specified productivity metrics.
## 3. What environment features actually move activity
### 3.1 Walkability and daily destinations
**MEASURED:** The IPEN cross-sectional study measured accelerometer MVPA in 6,822 adults aged 18–66 from 14 cities in 10 countries. Net residential density, intersection density, public-transport density and number of parks were positively associated with activity; the estimated gap between the most and least activity-friendly neighbourhoods was **68–89 minutes/week**, 45%–59% of the 150-minute guideline. \[10\]
**MEASURED — null result:** In that same IPEN study, mixed land use and distance to the nearest public-transport point were not related to activity after modelling. This does not show that land-use mix never matters; it shows the variables did not independently predict measured activity in that dataset. \[10\]
**MEASURED, observational:** A longitudinal-review synthesis found public transport availability most consistently associated with overall activity/walking. It also reported sex/gender patterning: safety and cycle lanes were more relevant for women in the reviewed evidence, whereas road-network characteristics were more relevant for men. The evidence is not a standardised causal effect per kilometre of sidewalk. \[11\]
**MEASURED — null result:** One large six-year cohort of 20,354 US women in that synthesis found increases in housing density and bus availability associated with more utilitarian walking, but land use, street connectivity, traffic, sidewalks and distance to parks were not associated with utilitarian or exercise walking. A separate child cohort found no significant association between change in commute/home-environment supportiveness and overall activity. These counterexamples matter: plausible urban-design features do not reliably translate into total activity. \[11\]
### 3.2 Cycling and walking infrastructure
**MEASURED, natural experiment:** The UK iConnect study followed residents near three new traffic-free routes. Proximity did **not** predict activity change after one year. At two years, each kilometre closer was associated with **15.3 additional minutes/week** of walking and cycling (95% CI 6.5–24.2) and **12.5 additional minutes/week** total activity (1.9–23.1). Effects were larger among route users: 30.0 minutes/week (3.5–55.5), while the estimate for non-users was 7.4 (−5.3 to 20.1). \[13\]
**MEASURED, with substantial limitations:** iConnect used a cohort/natural-experiment design, not random allocation; the outcomes were self-reported; only 1,465 people remained at two years from 22,500 initially mailed. It supports a credible, delayed local activity increase from high-quality route infrastructure, not a universal per-kilometre effect. \[13\]
**MEASURED, evidence synthesis:** The 2022 umbrella review counted 120 walking/cycling built-environment changes: **54.2% positive**, **41.7% null** and **4.2% negative** for use or combined activity. Cycling-specific changes were 61.1% positive and 33.3% null; combined walking-and-cycling changes were 69.2% positive and 23.1% null. These are counts of heterogeneous changes, not a pooled causal effect size. \[12\]
**Interpretation:** **MIXED EVIDENCE.** A connected, safe route which people actually use can raise activity, but a route is not interchangeable with a health outcome. Network connectivity, trip purpose, safety, access, promotion, car ownership and maintenance are potential effect modifiers; their Dongjiakou values are **UNKNOWN**.
### 3.3 Green space, parks and outdoor equipment
**MEASURED, umbrella review:** Across 62 park/playground changes, 59.7% were positive, 33.9% null and 6.5% negative for use or combined activity. New pocket parks were positive in 7/8 changes; park renovation in 16/24; and added exercise equipment in 5/8. The underlying reviews contain heterogeneous outcomes and designs; these proportions are a direction-of-findings count, not an average gain. \[12\]
**MEASURED, China-specific review:** A systematic review of 41 studies in China found street greenness and access to green space associated with walking, active commuting, activity and use in many studies. It also found null associations for several features: natural-environment size, landscape quality, attractiveness, park features and safety in individual studies; park size was not associated with activity frequency or weekly duration in one study. Mean study-quality score was 6/14; only two studies had a follow-up long enough to observe change, and none measured exposure before outcomes. Causal evidence in China is therefore limited. \[16\]
**Interpretation:** **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED.** Green and attractive space is a credible enabling condition with measured associations and some natural-experiment support. The popular claim that merely adding greenery, a plaza or outdoor equipment reliably shifts whole-population activity is weaker than its reputation. Design, accessibility, programs and user needs are central, and their local performance is **UNKNOWN**.
### 3.4 Proximity to facilities
**MEASURED:** In IPEN, number of parks within network buffers was positively associated with accelerometer-measured activity (exponentiated coefficient 1.146, 95% CI 1.033–1.272), while distance to nearest transport was not. The study measures associations across existing neighbourhoods and cannot disentangle people selecting activity-friendly locations from the environment changing their behaviour. \[10\]
**MEASURED:** In the China green-space review, shorter distance/access to green space was generally associated with more activity or use; nevertheless, individual studies found no association for proximate greenways and several park attributes. The correct evidence status for an unmeasured Dongjiakou facility service radius is **UNKNOWN**, not an assumed benefit. \[16\]
## 4. Provision versus behaviour change: what moves a population, rather than an already active individual
**MEASURED:** Objective behavioural interventions have measurable but small long-term effects. At 24 months, four RCTs (2,347 people) found MVPA SMD **0.18** (0.07–0.29; I²=41%); four trials (2,410 people) found steps/day SMD **0.15** (0.02–0.28). At 36–48 months, MVPA SMD was **0.16** (0.09–0.23; 3,435 people). The authors converted the 24-month MVPA SMD to **MODELED** 21–25 minutes/week using assumed standard deviations; it is not a directly observed common-minute difference across all trials. \[14\]
**MEASURED:** The same review could not identify a behavioural component clearly associated with success across the included trials. It supports behaviour-change support as a modest average lever, not a guaranteed protocol. \[14\]
**MEASURED — strong population-level null result:** Cochrane reviewed 33 multi-strategy community-wide programmes in 267 communities. The four new low-risk-of-bias studies did not increase population physical activity; one accelerometer study observed more walking at programme level but no change in seven-day average MVPA across the community. The review concluded that better-designed studies generally showed no population-level improvement, while reporting costs and sustainability was limited. \[15\]
**MEASURED:** The evidence synthesis of environmental change is more favourable for changing **use, active travel or combined activity** than for proving a total-population health effect. It contains both positive and null results at high frequency. \[12\]
**Direct answer:** **Infrastructure provision can enable and sometimes increase walking, cycling or park use, particularly when routes/parks are genuinely used; it does not by itself have a reliably quantified population-health effect. Behavioural programmes move objectively measured activity by small average amounts over 2–4 years. The literature does not support a fixed “infrastructure versus behaviour change” conversion factor.** The defensible approach is to treat them as complementary, measure both reach and actual activity, and include a comparator rather than claim an effect from facility count alone.
## 5. China and Qingdao: targets, provision and outcomes
### National Fitness Plan (2021–2025)
**REPORTED:** The State Council’s plan set a 2025 target of **38.5%** of people regularly participating in physical exercise, full coverage of county/township/community public fitness facilities and community 15-minute fitness circles, and **2.16 social sports instructors per 1,000 people**. It also called for new or refurbished **2,000+** sports parks, fitness centres or public sports venues; equipment in **5,000+** townships/subdistricts; and digital upgrading of **1,000+** public venues. These are policy targets, not independently measured causal outcomes. \[17\]
**REPORTED:** National reporting in 2025 stated that sports-ground area reached **3.0 m²/person** by December 2024, exceeding the 2.6 m² Fourteenth Five-Year facility target, and that regular participation reached **37.2%** by September 2024 against the 38.5% target. The report also identifies unequal access and quality as continuing concerns. It does not report changes in mortality, CVD, diabetes, depression, cognition, absence or productivity attributable to the plan. \[18\]
**Assessment:** **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED AS PROVISION/UPTAKE.** The programme has measured/reported coverage and participation indicators. A causal national population-health outcome is **UNKNOWN** from these sources. Reaching an area-per-person target is not evidence of an equal distribution, high quality, safe access, regular use or health effect.
### Qingdao relevance
**REPORTED:** Qingdao’s 2021 sports development plan reported regular participation of **49.5%**, more than 10,000 fitness facilities, 3.29 m² sports-ground area/person, about 5,400 fitness guidance stations, and a historical five-year investment of nearly **¥300 million** for 550+ new facilities and 2,000+ upgrades. It sets 2025 targets of 3.5 m²/person, 50% regular participation and 95.5% passing the national physical-fitness standard. \[19\]
**REPORTED:** A Xinhua report on the same historical programme said the roughly ¥300 million covered a mix of fitness facilities over five years, including 686 primarily caged-football/multifunction fields, plus later upgrades and parks. It did not allocate expenditure by facility type, report an actual utilisation denominator, or provide a per-capita capital/lifecycle cost. \[20\]
**Assessment:** **REPORTED, not causal evaluation.** These are useful context and monitoring baselines for Qingdao, but they cannot show that the facilities caused the participation or fitness figures, and they do not provide an evidence base specific to Dongjiakou’s industrial, shift-working and residential population.

## 7. Mental health, social cohesion, active sport and spectator sport
**MEASURED, mostly observational:** A 2023 systematic review of sport participation in adults included 29 studies (ages 18–84). It found sport participation associated with better self-rated mental health, mood, life satisfaction, self-esteem, health-related quality of life and social outcomes, and team sport generally associated with better outcomes than individual sport or other activity. The review did not pool a defensible common effect size because outcomes and designs were heterogeneous. \[23\]
**MEASURED, evidence-quality warning:** In that review, most evidence was cross-sectional or observational; only six controlled interventions were identified, and none of the three nominal RCTs reported adequate randomisation, allocation concealment or blinding. The authors identify self-selection and heterogeneity as important limitations. Thus, “sport builds cohesion and mental health” has a real association evidence base, but the causal investment claim is **MIXED EVIDENCE**, not settled fact. \[23\]
**UNKNOWN:** A measured incremental mental-health or cohesion benefit of team sport over an equally social, equally active non-sport alternative in a general industrial-node population is not available from the reviewed evidence. Potential harms from injury, exclusion, poor coaching or competition were not adequately quantified in the cited adult review. \[23\]
**MEASURED, scoping rather than causal synthesis:** A scoping study identified 135 empirical papers on spectator sport and population health from 1990–2014. It maps possible physical, mental and social pathways, but its authors state that the direction and size of spectator-sport effects require better evidence and systematic synthesis. It is not a controlled estimate of community cohesion, clinical mental health or population activity created by a stadium or team. \[24\]
**Assessment of spectator sport:** **MIXED EVIDENCE.** Spectatorship can plausibly create shared identity and social interaction, and some individual studies report well-being associations. The popular claim that spectator facilities deliver a measurable population-health dividend is weaker than its reputation. A Dongjiakou spectator-sport health effect size, distributional effect, and net effect after crowding, cost and displacement are **UNKNOWN**.

**Minimum evidence design before any Dongjiakou health/productivity claim — UNKNOWN until implemented:** a baseline and follow-up representative activity measure (preferably accelerometry subsample plus validated survey); facility use counts disaggregated by sex, age, disability, worker/resident status and shift; route safety and accessibility audit; register-based sickness absence with a comparable workforce/site; pre-specified mental-health and cohesion instruments; injury/incident monitoring; capital, operating and renewal costs by asset; and an appropriate comparison area or phased rollout. ## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN
1. **UNKNOWN:** Dongjiakou’s current activity distribution, active-travel mode share, recreational sport participation, physical-fitness status and unmet demand by worker/resident subgroup.
2. **UNKNOWN:** Whether workers at the port, steel plant, chemical park, food-processing park, data centre and desalination facility can safely and practically use facilities across actual shifts, including women, older workers, temporary workers, people with disabilities and nearby residents.
3. **UNKNOWN:** The local causal effect of walkability, protected cycle routes, green space, facility proximity, operating hours, price, programming or coaching on total activity. International and China-wide associations are not local measurements.
4. **UNKNOWN:** Dongjiakou baseline rates of all-cause mortality, CVD, diabetes, depression and cognitive decline; hence absolute cases prevented, health-care savings and quality-of-life years cannot be calculated without unsupported assumptions.
5. **UNKNOWN:** Causal productivity, presenteeism, absenteeism and retention benefit from any specific facility or programme in heavy industry/port work. The cited workplace evidence cannot be transferred as a fixed financial return.
