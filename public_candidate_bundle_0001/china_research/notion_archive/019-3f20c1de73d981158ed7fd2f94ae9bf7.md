## 1. You caught a real error, and it is mine
The challenge is correct, and it exposes a contradiction I introduced one step earlier.
I built `models/valuation.py` to encode exactly this insight: **market price is not value, and market value decays under replication while strategic offset does not.** Then, in the very next document, I used **"market-breaking" as a reason not to produce.**
Those two positions cannot both be held. If market price is not the measure of value, then a collapsing price is not a reason to stop — it is a statement about *price*, not about *worth*. The phrase "market-breaking" crept in as a verdict when it is only an observation.
**And worse, it inverted the sign of the effect.** If the lattice has the lowest marginal cost, a price collapse is not a loss. It is a **competitive win** — it bankrupts higher-cost producers and the lattice takes the market. That is the standard commodity play, and I recorded it as a liability.
**Concretely:** "120 nodes would be 772–858% of Chinese magnesium production" is a true statement about *quantity*. It was reported as a blocker. It should have been reported as **abundance**, with the question then being *what to do with it* — which is what you asked.

### 3.5 Stockpiling and slow-drip export
You raised both, and both are legitimate — with one caveat each.
**Stockpiling is sound and China already does it.** Strategic reserves exist precisely for materials whose domestic production is vulnerable. The cost is storage and capital tie-up, which for stable inorganic minerals is modest. The option value is real: a reserve bought at near-zero marginal cost is cheap insurance. **The register should record it as a legitimate disposition route, not a failure to sell.**
**Slow-drip export is sound in principle, with a real friction:** sustained sales below cost invite **anti-dumping actions**, which convert a commercial strategy into a trade dispute. The mitigation is to sell at or above fully-allocated cost and let volume be capped by the market rather than by intent — which is compatible with everything above, since the carbon and substitution uses absorb volume the market will not.

## 4. The fifth valuation basis
Your challenge requires the register to name something it could not previously express: **a material can be consumed to produce a benefit, rather than sold.**
I am adding it:
```javascript
PUBLIC_GOOD   the material is consumed to produce a non-market benefit —
              carbon mineralised, a high-emission incumbent displaced,
              an ecosystem service delivered.
              Does NOT decay under replication. In fact it SCALES with
              replication, because the benefit is bounded by the carbon
              or substitution opportunity, not by a market.
```
The four existing bases are `MARKET_VALUE`, `STRATEGIC_OFFSET`, `REMOVAL_VALUE` and `POLICY_VALUE`. `PUBLIC_GOOD` is distinct from `REMOVAL_VALUE` — removal is mass taken *out of the receiving environment*, while public good is what the material *does downstream* once it leaves.
**And this basis inverts the replication logic.** Market value decays with replication. Strategic offset is bounded by national demand. **Public good is bounded by the problem it solves** — and the carbon problem is far larger than any market the lattice could saturate. That is the answer to "isn't massive production an opportunity rather than a detriment": **on this basis, more nodes means more benefit with no ceiling in sight.**

## 5. The reframe, stated plainly
> **Cheap material stops being a product and becomes a building material.**
Aluminium was more valuable than gold before the Hall–Héroult process. Electrolysis made it cheap, and the response to cheapness was not to restrict production — it was to **fly in it**. Every structural material follows that path: the price falls, and the uses multiply to fill the abundance.
The question you are asking is the right one, and it is the question that comes *after* the one I was answering. I was answering *"will the market absorb it?"* — which is a question about price. You are asking *"what should we build with it?"* — which is a question about use.
**So the corrected position is:**
1. **Market-breaking is not a prohibition.** It is an observation about price. It must never again appear as a reason not to produce.
2. **The real constraint is energy allocation**, not market absorption — and that constraint may dissolve if heat integration works.
3. **The strongest disposition is consumption for public good**, above all CO₂ mineralisation: 105,356 t CO₂/yr at one node, 12.6 Mt/yr at 120 nodes, needing no buyer.
4. **Stockpiling and cost-floor exports are legitimate**, with anti-dumping as the only real friction.
5. **Where a material has no such use at scale** — bromine is the honest example — that is a genuine finding, and it should be stated as a *use* limitation, not a *market* one.

## 6. What remains unknown
- **Whether heat integration works.** This is now the switch for the whole abundance argument. If compute waste heat drives crystallisation, marginal cost approaches zero and §2's qualification dissolves.
- **The actual marginal cost per tonne** for each mineral at node scale. Every figure here is `MODELED` from published ranges.
- **Whether mineral carbonation is kinetically and thermodynamically practical on this brine** at this scale, and what the reagent demand is. The research pass confirmed the pathway exists (57% conversion, TRL 5–6) but not at this site.
- **Whether Cl₂ from the salt route has any viable disposition.** If it does not, that route is closed regardless of the NaOH benefit.
- **Bromine's genuine use ceiling.** This is the one material where the honest answer may be that abundance has no outlet, and it should be tested rather than assumed.

That answer does not need a market, and it does not decay when the lattice grows.
