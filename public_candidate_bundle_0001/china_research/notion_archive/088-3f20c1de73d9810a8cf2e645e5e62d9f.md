## Executive summary
- **MODELED — principal finding.** With the stated **60,000 mg/L concentrate** and **35,000 mg/L seawater**, the ideal-NaCl, 25 °C Gibbs-energy upper bound is only **0.184 kWh per m³ of concentrate** when the concentrate is equilibrated against an effectively infinite seawater reservoir. At the stated **18–22 million m³/y** concentrate volume, that is **3.31–4.04 GWh/y before all conversion and pumping losses**, or **0.47–0.58% of the stated 700 GWh/y mineral-recovery electrical load**. It is therefore **too small to matter as a strategic energy offset** for that load. Source: calculation below; thermodynamic framing \[S1\]; flow/load are REPORTED task-context inputs \[S0\].
- **MODELED.** A finite, equal-volume mixer releases only **0.084 kWh/m³-concentrate** under the same ideal-NaCl model, or **1.51–1.84 GWh/y**. The term “energy available from mixing” has no unique per-m³ answer unless the seawater-to-concentrate flow ratio and final state are specified. Source: calculation below; \[S1\]; \[S0\].
- **MEASURED.** No measured net energy-recovery fraction has been found for the *specific* 60/35 g/L concentrate/seawater pairing. The closest RED evidence uses RO concentrate with low-salinity surface water, not seawater, and reported **0.96 W/m² gross** with natural RO brine; a cited bench test reported **17–26% energy efficiency** with RO brine and sewage discharge. Those results cannot be transferred to the weaker 60/35 g/L gradient. Source: \[S2\].
- **MEASURED.** Real-brine RED pilots have reported **1.6 W/m²** over five months for brackish water plus saturated brine (Italy) and **0.96 W/m² gross** for natural RO-brine plus surface water (Japan). Both are pilot scale, not commercial-scale power plants. Source: \[S3\]; \[S2\].
- **MEASURED / REPORTED.** A Singapore laboratory-scale SWRO–PRO pilot reported **5.0–5.7 W/m²** at 0.8 M brine and 15 bar with tap water or wastewater-retentate feeds; its cleaning trials required chemical cleaning for full recovery. A Japanese **460 m³/d** concentrate / **420 m³/d** treated-wastewater demonstration reported **10 W/m²** and one year without permeate-flow decline, but the report is authored by the operating company and gives no independently auditable net-kWh result. Neither is a 60/35 g/L seawater-feed result. Source: \[S4\]; \[S5\].
- **REPORTED / MEASURED.** The leading named developers found are REDstack (Netherlands), Toyobo and Kyowakiden (Japan), and the former Statkraft programme (Norway). The current Afsluitdijk site calls itself a **test plant** and says scale-up is still needed for market use; Statkraft stopped PRO investment in 2013 because it did not expect competitiveness in the foreseeable future. Source: \[S6\]; \[S7\]; \[S8\].
- **MODELED / REPORTED.** A 2024 PRO techno-economic model using 70,000-ppm brine and 42,000-ppm seawater found only **7% energy saving** versus its modelled SWRO base case and assumed a five-year module life; it estimated viable PRO membrane costs below **US\$2.2/m²**, versus provider quotations of **US\$9.2–12.3/m²**. This is a model with assumptions, not a Dongjiakou result. Source: \[S9\].
- **MEASURED / MODELED.** The concentrate can be a **salt source** for a salt-gradient solar pond, but a pond’s heat-storage layer is normally near saturation and requires salt/high-concentration-brine replenishment; 60 g/L is therefore not, by itself, a demonstrated direct substitute for that layer. An osmotic heat engine is a separate low-grade-heat technology using a regenerable draw solute (not an energy recovery of the NaCl/seawater gradient). Source: \[S10\]; \[S11\].

No original analytical chemistry, flow record, discharge temperature, or operating-hour record was supplied with the task. Source: \[S0\].

A reported result is not treated as independently verified merely because it appears in a company-authored paper.
## 1. Thermodynamic quantity: what can be stated rigorously
### 1.1 The exact number is UNKNOWN from TDS alone
**UNKNOWN.** An exact Gibbs free energy of mixing for the node cannot be calculated from two TDS values alone. It requires, at minimum, the dissolved-ion composition, ionic activities/activity coefficients at the discharge temperature, density, the selected amount of seawater, and the final mixing state. TDS is not a chemical speciation measurement. Source: \[S1\] explains that reversible PRO work equals the Gibbs free energy of mixing; task context supplies TDS only \[S0\].
**MODELED.** The transparent calculation below is therefore an **ideal, fully dissociated NaCl–water approximation at 25 °C**. It is a screening upper-bound model, not a measurement of Qingdao concentrate. Treating all TDS as NaCl, using ideal activities, and assuming a 1,000 kg mass for each nominal cubic metre are explicit assumptions. Source: thermodynamic maximum relationship \[S1\]; inputs \[S0\].

\[

\[
mathbf n_H=(1\{,\}026.65,1\{,\}026.65,52\{,\}177.93),qquad

\[
G(mathbf n)=RTsum_i n_iln x_i,qquad x_i=frac\{n_i\}\{sum_j n_j\}

## 2. RED: actual evidence, recoverable fraction, and scale
### 2.1 What RED has actually demonstrated
**MEASURED — pilot scale, Italy.** Tedesco et al. operated a 125-cell-pair RED unit with about 50 m² ion-exchange membrane area for five months on real brackish water (\~0.03 M NaCl equivalent) and saturated brine (4–5 M NaCl equivalent). It reached about **40 W, 1.6 W/m² per cell pair** on natural feeds and **65 W, 2.7 W/m²** on artificial NaCl feeds. The authors reported no significant performance loss from scaling, fouling, or ageing in that particular five-month test. This is **pilot scale**, and its salinity contrast is far stronger than 60/35 g/L. Source: \[S3\].
**MEASURED — pilot scale, Japan.** Mehdizadeh et al. installed a 299-cell-pair, 179.4-m² RED stack at an Okinawa seawater-desalination plant. Natural RO brine (\~1 M NaCl) against nearby surface water gave a maximum **0.96 W/m² gross**; model RO brine/surface water gave **1.46 W/m² gross** and about **1.29 W/m² net** at the authors’ optimum flow condition. Natural divalent ions increased resistance/concentration polarization, and the RO brine was sand-filtered and passed through a 0.45-µm cartridge filter before the stack. This is **pilot scale**, and the low-salinity feed was surface water, not 35 g/L seawater. Source: \[S2\].
**MEASURED — laboratory/bench scale, not target pairing.** The same paper cites a bench-scale RO-brine/sewage-discharge study with **17–26% energy efficiency**. This is the closest reported recovery fraction found for real RO concentrate, but it is **not commercial scale** and not the 60/35 g/L pairing. Source: \[S2\].
**UNKNOWN — target practical recovery.** No source found reports a net electrical recovery fraction, annual kWh/m³, membrane area, or long-duration fouling result for **60,000 mg/L concentrate mixed with 35,000 mg/L seawater** in a RED plant. Therefore a practical RED recoverable fraction for Dongjiakou is **UNKNOWN**. Source: evidence gap after review of \[S2\], \[S3\], \[S6\], \[S12\].
### 2.2 Fouling and real concentrate
**MEASURED.** In the Italian real-brine pilot, five months of operation did not show significant scaling, fouling, or ageing. That result is encouraging but limited to its feeds, pretreatment, membrane stack, and test duration; it does not establish a membrane lifetime. Source: \[S3\].
**MEASURED.** The same paper reports that, in an earlier natural freshwater/seawater RED experiment with only a 20-µm filter, power fell **40% during the first day**; colloidal/organic material, including clay minerals and diatom silica shells, was identified as the cause. The authors identify periodic air sparging and feed switching as proposed mitigation, not as a proof of lifetime. Source: \[S3\].
**MEASURED.** In the Okinawa pilot, multivalent ions in natural brine depressed output versus the model solution, and the feed underwent RO pretreatment plus sand and cartridge filtration before RED. This demonstrates that “real concentrate” is not equivalent to NaCl brine and that upstream treatment becomes part of the energy balance. Source: \[S2\].
### 2.3 State of deployment and economics
**REPORTED — developer source; pilot, not commercial.** REDstack states that its Afsluitdijk facility began producing 50 kW in 2015. The regional Afsluitdijk project authority currently describes the facility as a **test plant**, says it must demonstrate effectiveness at larger configuration and for end users, and identifies future expansion and market availability as the next steps. The conflicting descriptions mean that the safe classification is **demonstration/test**, not established commercial utility-scale generation. Source: \[S7\]; \[S6\].
**MODELED / REPORTED.** IRENA’s 2014 technology brief estimated dedicated salinity-gradient membranes at EUR10–30/m², required EUR2–5/m² for competitiveness, and projected membrane replacement/maintenance over five years. Those are dated projections, not present quotations or demonstrated lifetime. A 2024 RED review reports a membrane price around EUR4.3/m² and a predicted EUR0.17/kWh RED case, but this is a review’s cost scenario rather than a transparent supplier quote for a specific high-salinity, real-concentrate stack. Source: \[S6\]; \[S13\].
**UNKNOWN.** A documented, independently measured membrane lifetime and delivered membrane price for a RED stack treating Dongjiakou-like concentrate are not found. The evidence does **not** justify substituting generic RO/ED membrane lifetimes for this application. Source: gaps in \[S2\], \[S3\], \[S6\], \[S13\].
## 3. PRO: actual evidence, recoverable fraction, and scale
### 3.1 What PRO has actually demonstrated
**MEASURED — laboratory-scale pilot, Singapore.** Lee et al. tested 1-inch TFC hollow-fibre modules for 565 operating hours. With 0.8 M (46,750 mg/L) synthetic or real SWRO brine at 15 bar and tap water or wastewater retentate, they reported **5.0–5.7 W/m²**. This is a laboratory-scale pilot with substantially fresher feeds than ambient seawater. It does not establish the result for 60/35 g/L. Source: \[S4\].
**REPORTED — demonstration scale, Japan; interested-party evidence.** Company-affiliated authors report a 460 m³/d concentrated-brine / 420 m³/d treated-sewage demonstration with eight 10-inch Toyobo CTA hollow-fibre modules and a 7.7-kW-rated Pelton turbine. They report **10 W/m²** and no permeate-flow decline over a continuous year. The paper does not provide an independently auditable net electrical output or a net energy-per-m³ figure, and turbine rating must not be treated as measured plant output. This is a **demonstration test site**, not evidence of commercial deployment. Source: \[S5\].
**MEASURED — historical pilot.** IRENA reported that Statkraft’s 2,000-m² PRO pilot could technically produce 10 kW but actually produced around 5 kW. Statkraft later said it had proven the technology worked but stopped investment in 2013 because it did not expect competitive cost in the foreseeable future. Source: \[S6\]; \[S8\].
**UNKNOWN — target practical recovery.** No field result found gives a net recovery fraction or annual kWh/m³ for PRO using **60,000 mg/L brine as draw solution and 35,000 mg/L seawater as feed**. The target pairing is materially weaker than brine/freshwater and brine/wastewater demonstrations. A practical PRO recoverable fraction for this node is **UNKNOWN**. Source: evidence gap after review of \[S4\], \[S5\], \[S9\].
### 3.2 What “91% efficiency” does and does not mean
**MODELED — not a field result and not target pairing.** Yip and Elimelech’s constant-pressure PRO calculation for seawater/river water gave 0.75 kWh/m³ versus 0.81 kWh/m³ reversible work, or **91.1% thermodynamic extraction efficiency**. The paper also shows that high power-density operation conflicts with maximum energy extraction. This is a process model using seawater/river water; it is **not** a practical recovery factor for 60/35 g/L or a commercial availability claim. Source: \[S1\].
**MODELED — target-relevant but not measured.** A 2024 bench/model study used 70,000-ppm brine draw and 42,000-ppm seawater feed at 35 °C. It selected 20-bar draw pressure and predicted **7% energy saving** in its modelled 2,000-m³/d SWRO configuration. It assumed a five-year PRO life and concluded that economic viability at US\$0.16/kWh electricity required module cost below US\$2.2/m², while personal communications with providers gave US\$9.2–12.3/m². This is relevant in direction—moderate brine/seawater gradients give low value—but must not be used as a measured Dongjiakou recovery rate. Source: \[S9\].
### 3.3 Fouling and membrane life
**MEASURED — laboratory-scale pilot.** With real SWRO brine and wastewater retentate, acid maintenance cleaning in the Singapore PRO pilot fully recovered water flux/power density; caustic cleaning recovered up to 80%, and chemical-free backwash/CIP recovered only 42–51%. The authors found intermittent CIP slowed flux decline. This is direct evidence that fouling control, chemical use, and downtime are real parasitic considerations. Source: \[S4\].
**REPORTED — company-affiliated demonstration.** The Japanese demonstration authors say the same modules had no permeate-flow decline after one year. This is a useful reported long-duration observation, but it is not an independently replicated lifetime result and uses treated sewage rather than ambient seawater. Source: \[S5\].
**UNKNOWN.** A demonstrated five-year membrane lifetime in a real brine/seawater PRO service has not been found. The five-year life in the target-relevant techno-economic analysis is an **assumption**, not an observed life. Source: \[S9\].
## 4. Cross-technology judgment
**MODELED / MEASURED.** RED and PRO can technically turn salinity-gradient free energy into electricity, but published performance does not bridge the gap between high-gradient brine/freshwater demonstrations and the node’s modest 60/35 g/L gradient. Real feeds introduce multivalent-ion transport, organic/colloidal fouling, pressure drop, cleaning, pretreatment, and membrane replacement. Source: \[S2\]; \[S3\]; \[S4\]; \[S12\].
**REPORTED / MODELED.** Literature is not uniformly optimistic. IRENA’s 2014 brief projected hybrid applications could benefit from shared infrastructure, while also calling cost evidence sparse and its estimates speculative. A 2024 critical perspective concluded that salinity-gradient energy is unlikely to become mainstream/cost-competitive because of intrinsically low energy density and conversion efficiency; it states that even optimistic PRO competitiveness needs brine above 18% salinity. The node’s reported 6% concentrate is far below that modelled threshold. Source: \[S6\]; \[S14\]; \[S0\].
**Decision:** **Do not count RED or PRO electricity from mixing this concentrate with ambient seawater as a material offset against the node’s 700 GWh/y mineral-recovery demand.** At most, retain it as a research-stage brine-management or demonstration option after the node supplies full brine chemistry, intake/outfall hydraulics, pretreatment energy, membrane quotations, and a measured pilot net-energy balance. This decision is based on the thermodynamic ceiling, not on an assumption that engineering will achieve it. Source: calculation above; \[S2\]–\[S6\]; \[S9\]; \[S14\].
## 5. Salt-gradient solar ponds and osmotic heat engines
### 5.1 Salt-gradient solar pond
**MEASURED — semi-pilot/laboratory, Pakistan, not commercial scale.** Manzoor et al. built a 4.65-m² experimental salt-gradient solar pond coupled to laboratory direct-contact membrane distillation. It extracted maximum temperatures of 37 °C in summer and 28.5 °C in winter. The authors state that desalination waste brine can be a salt source, but their lower convective zone is near saturation and requires regular addition of solid salt or high-concentration brine to compensate salt loss. Source: \[S10\].
**MODELED / UNKNOWN.** The 60 g/L concentrate can therefore be considered a **feedstock salt source**, but it cannot be credited as a directly usable lower-convective-zone fluid without further concentration or added salt. Whether a Dongjiakou pond has adequate land, evaporation control, solar resource, liner integrity, source-water management, seasonal thermal output, or economic advantage is **UNKNOWN**. A solar pond collects/stores **solar heat**; it does not recover the 60/35 g/L Gibbs energy as electricity. Source: \[S10\]; thermal-zone mechanism \[S15\].
### 5.2 Osmotic heat engine
**MEASURED / MODELED — laboratory concept, not commercial scale.** McGinnis et al. describe a closed-cycle osmotic heat engine (OHE) using concentrated ammonia–carbon-dioxide draw solution and nearly deionized working water. The paper models more than 200 W/m² membrane power density and likely 5–10% of Carnot efficiency, with heat as low as 40 °C in a 20 °C ambient case. It explicitly distinguishes this from natural-salinity-gradient harvesting: the source energy is low-grade heat used to regenerate the working solution. Source: \[S11\].
**MODELED / UNKNOWN.** Node waste heat could be assessed as a heat source for an OHE **separately** from the RO-concentrate salinity gradient. Using NaCl brine in thermally regenerated OHE concepts faces high heat input for water/solute separation and poor thermal efficiency; no commercial-scale NaCl-brine OHE at Dongjiakou-relevant conditions was found. Do not double-count heat exergy or claim that the RO concentrate itself supplies OHE electricity. Source: \[S11\].
## 6. China deployment, policy, and integration edges
### China deployment
**UNKNOWN.** No reliable public evidence was found of a **commercial-scale Chinese RED or PRO electricity plant** operating on RO concentrate/ambient seawater. China has ocean-energy policy support, but policy support is not evidence that this technology is commercially deployed. Source: \[S16\]; evidence gap after review of \[S2\]–\[S9\].
### Policy alignment and approvals
**REPORTED.** The IEA Ocean Energy Systems China profile says China’s Energy Law encourages large-scale ocean-energy development, that ocean-energy projects may participate in green-power trading, and that project approvals include EIA, sea-area rights, and grid connection. It does not state a salinity-gradient-specific feed-in tariff or a specific tariff rate. Source: \[S16\].
**REPORTED.** China’s marine-project regulation requires an environmental impact assessment, including forecast/assessment of marine-environment effects and protection measures, and requires a new report after major change to a project’s nature, scale, site, technology, or environmental protections. Altering a concentrate discharge by inserting RED/PRO, changing discharge salinity/temperature/flow, or building a pond/outfall is therefore likely to require project-specific regulatory review; exact Qingdao pathway is **UNKNOWN**. Source: \[S17\].

## WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN
1. **Exact node Gibbs energy:** full ionic composition, activities, density, alkalinity, pH, temperature, suspended solids, organics, oil/grease, microbiology, and seasonal variation for concentrate and receiving seawater.
2. **Hydraulic definition:** seawater-to-concentrate mixing ratio, permissible intake and discharge volumes, mixing-zone limits, pumping head, pipeline length, and outfall dilution requirements.
3. **Target-pair performance:** measured RED or PRO net kWh/m³, net power density, membrane area, efficiency, and duration for 60/35 g/L feeds; none was found.
4. **Fouling/lifetime:** site-specific cleaning frequency, chemical use, scaling indices, membrane replacement interval, module warranty, and delivered membrane/stack quotations.
5. **Economics:** installed CAPEX, OPEX, pretreatment/pumping energy, electrical interconnection cost, and a bankable LCOE; no defensible nutrient/product cost applies because this is an electricity process.
6. **China commercial deployment:** independently verifiable commercial-scale RED/PRO electricity operation on this type of feed was not found.
7. **Policy details:** Qingdao/Shandong tariff, green-certificate eligibility, local EIA/sea-use pathway, discharge-consent changes, and whether a demonstration receives support.
8. **Solar pond:** coastal land area, local solar irradiation, evaporation/rainfall balance, concentration method, liner/land-use constraints, thermal load, and brine quality compatibility.
9. **OHE:** usable waste-heat temperature/flow profile, heat-exchanger approach temperatures, working-fluid containment and regeneration cost, and a commercial deployment benchmark.
