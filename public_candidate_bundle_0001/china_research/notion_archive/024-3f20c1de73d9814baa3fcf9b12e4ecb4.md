## 0. The one architectural gap that explains why net positive keeps receding
The register models two things at the boundary: **what the node takes** and **what the node returns**. Every stream is a take or a return.
Under that model, the best achievable outcome is *returns that are not worse than the takes*. That is what `INV-19` enforces. It is why the ecology conversation has been stuck on "no measurable harm" — because **a take-and-return model has no term that can produce a benefit.**
**Net positive requires a third term: what the node removes from the boundary.**
I would add a third stream class to the register:
```javascript
INTERCEPTED_FROM_BOUNDARY   mass the node takes OUT of the environment
                            and does not put back
```
This is not a bookkeeping nicety. It changes what is measurable. A discharge can only ever be argued down toward zero harm. An **interception is a positive number**, verifiable by intake-minus-outfall differential, and it accumulates. It is the only term that can make the boundary net better rather than merely not-worse.
Your instinct — "replenishment is not discharge" — is pointing at exactly this. But the register cannot adopt the *label*; labels have to follow evidence, or we are back to assertion. What it can adopt is the **mechanism**: a discharge becomes replenishment when it has been demonstrated to leave the receiving system better than it found it, and that requires removals, not vocabulary.

## 4. The intake is the monitoring station that does not exist
**This unblocks ****`ECO-01`****, and it is the highest-leverage idea here.**
`ECO-01` is the register's critical path, and it is blocked for a specific reason: no primary station-level ecological data exists publicly, and the 2017/2022 Ocean University series returned `NULL`. The register has been waiting for someone else to build a monitoring programme.
**But the node already moves 35.86 Mm³/year of bay water through a monitored intake.** That is a continuous, flow-proportional, permanently instrumented sample of the receiving water — precisely the dataset `ECO-01` and `INV-20` require.
Instrumenting the intake gives you, continuously and as **primary** data:
- baseline water quality before any node effect, at high temporal resolution;
- intake-minus-outfall differential, which is the direct measurement of **what the node removes** (§0, §3);
- the seasonality that the brine brief lists as a sampling requirement;
- the counterfactual for any ecological claim.
**The strategic consequence:** `ECO-01`'s critical path moves from *"wait for a government monitoring programme"* to *"instrument what you already built."* That is a change from an unfunded external dependency to an internal engineering decision.
I would make intake instrumentation a **precondition** of any net-positive claim, not an optional extra. A claim that cannot be checked against a baseline is not a claim.

## 5. Carbon — two loops, one of which is genuinely large
**MODELED.** Node CO₂ ≈ **194,900 t/year** (40 MW compute at 0.5 kg CO₂/kWh ≈ 175,200 t; desalination at 2.2 kWh/m³ ≈ 19,700 t).
### 5.1 Brine mineralisation (`CARB01` ↔ `R01`)
Brine is rich in Ca (16,137 t/yr) and Mg (48,411 t/yr). Reacting it with captured CO₂ precipitates CaCO₃/MgCO₃ — **permanent** storage on a geological timescale, plus a saleable material, plus removal of the very ions that make the discharge problematic. This is the one loop in the register that is positive on **three** metrics simultaneously: carbon, marine chemistry, and materials.
### 5.2 Ocean alkalinity enhancement — and it may actually pencil out
Adding alkalinity to the brine counteracts local acidification and increases the seawater's CO₂ uptake capacity. **MODELED:** offsetting the node's full 194,900 t/yr on 17.93 Mm³ of brine requires **\~247 µmol/kg** added alkalinity — about **10.7% of natural seawater alkalinity (\~2,300 µmol/kg)**.
That is a *plausible* engineering target rather than an absurd one. **HYPOTHESIS:** a \~10% alkalinity enhancement on the brine could offset the node's own carbon footprint while improving the receiving water's carbonate chemistry.
**Two honest caveats, stated up front.** First, this is a local acidification measure, not a global CDR play — the brine volume is far too small to matter globally, and I would not let it be sold as carbon removal. Second, OAE durability, verification and ecological risk are actively contested in the literature, and the register's discipline means it stays `HYPOTHESIS` until measured.

## 6. Your pH instinct is right, and it generalises better than anything else here
**Optimal pH per locality** is a genuinely good idea, and it is cheap. Seawater pH is \~8.1; acidification is a real stressor; brine pH is tunable. Discharging at a locality-specific target — derived from local carbonate chemistry and the species present — is a lever that costs almost nothing.
**Why it matters for the lattice:** it is the clearest example of a parameter that **must not be global**. Every one of the 120 localities gets its own pH and alkalinity target, because every receiving water differs. That is a template for how the whole node type should handle local parameters, and it belongs in `NODE_TEMPLATE.md`.

## 7. Where I would redirect the uranium instinct
You raised radioactive material and fish stocks. I ran it, and the answer is uncomfortable but clear:
**MODELED: the intake contains about 0.12 kg of uranium per year.** 120 grams. At natural seawater concentration of \~3.3 µg/L, uranium is not anthropogenically elevated and is not a fish-stock threat — removing it would be a capability demonstration, not a remediation.
**The instinct behind it is correct and I would not drop it** — the node should be able to remove contaminants rather than merely avoid adding them. But the honest target list is different, and these are the ones where interception is both large and consequential:
- **microplastics** (§3) — by far the largest;
- **nutrients (N, P)** — coastal Chinese waters carry excess nutrient loads; capture pairs naturally with `BIO01` and with seaweed cultivation, which is a genuine carbon *and* nutrient sink;
- **heavy metals** (Hg, Cd, Pb) and **organotins** — bioaccumulating, and the real fish-stock risk;
- **PFAS and persistent organics** — if the chemical park's effluent reaches the intake;
- **radionuclides** — worth the capability, but the driver is the **steel plant and regional coal combustion** (coal ash concentrates uranium and thorium), not the sea. Monitor the *industrial* sources; the seawater baseline is not the problem.
I would rather give you this correction than let "uranium removal" appear in a plan where it cannot do the work being asked of it.
