## Source findings to attach to the US / China engineering comparison

1. Industrial stakeholders organized desal efforts beginning ~2014; public/industrial financing design emerged over subsequent years. Initial industry objective involved reliable supply and water-restriction exemptions.
2. City estimates cited in investigation: **2019: 10 MGD / ~$140 million**; **2020: 20 MGD / ~$220 million**; **early 2024: expansion planning ~$757 million**; later >$1 billion proposals, and city ended the then-current contractor relationship in September 2025. These are **different capacities, years and scopes**, not a pure same-design inflation time series.
3. Around early 2024, estimated **annual debt service ~$68 million** against an industrial drought-surcharge-exemption fee averaging **~$6 million annually**. Calculate funding mismatch subject to other available revenue, debt and customer accounts. No implication this $62M spread was the total project's ultimate deficit.
4. Article reports **at least $50 million spent** on desal preparation by October 2026 with no operating plant. Distinguish it from 2025 city reported ~$34.28M spent, from loan balances, and from subsequently voted-down / withdrawn design authorizations. The article also reports potential project exit obligations for ~$235M state borrowing plus ~$70M interest, which are financing consequences rather than additional paid construction costs.
5. Residents versus industry: initial assurances public would shoulder little cost became contested after redesigns. Local residents, industrial businesses and environmental advocates describe competing water-priority and bay ecology concerns. Do not presume universal support for a new desal plant; protect potable supply, rates, withdrawals and marine ecosystem in all ORCS scenarios.
6. Investigation attributes delays and conflict to **multiple causes**: COVID-19 disruptions, inflation, changing plans, regulatory review, leadership turnover and political disputes. **No inference of fraud, deliberate misconduct or $979M confirmed waste** from this article.
7. The reported Dongjiakou historic **~¥450M / 100,000 m3/day (~26.4 MGD)** versus Corpus proposed **$978.77M / 30 MGD** implies a ~12.8x **unadjusted nominal** capex-per-capacity ratio at illustrative Oct-2026 FX. NOT a verified comparable real-cost multiplier until normalize plant boundaries, distribution, marine structures, environmental standards, labor, currency/inflation and financing.

## ORCS model modifications suggested by evidence

- New contracted water **allocation/veto** matrix distinguishing household essential supply, industrial drought exempt volume, refinery reuse, municipal revenue and downstream ecological flow.
- Debt service coverage ratio by scenario, ring-fenced industrial purchase commitments, public revenue and residential burden.
- Chronological sunk-cost ledger: **actual paid, committed, proposed, canceled, liabilities/interest** as separate statuses and different units.
- Alternative procurement: identical delivered 30 MGD water quality/reliability, matched plant/intake/outfall/distribution and legislative/permit prerequisites; reference Dongjiakou as process benchmark, not transferred cost offer.
- QOL ledger: residential water/energy credits and tax-offset scenarios require legally available resources **after** utility debt, environmental mitigations and resilience reserves.
- Explicit model boundary: reporter investigation / city disclosures / engineering paper / local policy / synthetic ORCS assumptions separated; no realized Atlas field credit.
