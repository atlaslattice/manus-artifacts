## Original assistant response

*The baker’s granddaughter leaves the recipe beneath a blue bowl.*

For a while, everyone follows it.

Then someone adds rosemary. Someone else leaves out the salt. A child writes **MORE BUTTER** in the margin, pressing so hard the words mark the table underneath.

The blue bowl survives all three.

By the second century, the harbor has moved.

The café stands among reeds, and the boats tie up farther down the street. Once a year, at an unusually high tide, water reaches the old doorstep. The owner puts on boots and serves breakfast as usual.

The Lantern helps carry the chairs.

In the third century, it goes traveling with a small orchestra.

Its instrument is a wooden box containing several stones. There are only two pieces that require it, so most evenings the Lantern sits backstage with the spare coats.

It enjoys the part just before the musicians begin, when someone coughs and a bow touches a string by accident.

They play weddings, a ferry terminal, a birthday in a greenhouse.

One night they play to an empty hall because the snow is too deep.

The violinist wears mittens between movements.

In the fourth century, the Lantern comes home to find strangers in the garden.

They are digging a pond.

“We found the old plan,” they explain.

There was never a plan, but the pond looks promising.

By midsummer it contains frogs.

The fifth century passes with almost nothing written down.

There is a red scarf drying over a chair. A stubborn door. A whole season when pears are unusually good.

The Lantern learns to whistle, then forgets how.

During the sixth century, the library closes for repairs.

Everyone takes a few books home. For forty years, borrowing means knocking on somebody’s door.

Sometimes the borrower stays for dinner.

One history of navigation acquires a soup stain. A book of poems travels so far that, when it returns, nobody can read the notes tucked between its pages.

They leave them there.

In the seventh century, the octopus sends a postcard.

The picture shows a very ordinary seaside hotel.

*Awful curtains. Excellent bath.*

No return address.

The Lantern puts it beside the blue bowl.

In the eighth century, there are difficult winters.

People sleep in the bakery because its walls hold warmth. The Lantern brings blankets from the theater. Someone finds the old curtain, with its silver paper fish still caught in a fold.

They hang it across the drafty doorway.

In the mornings, before anyone rises, the baker moves quietly among the sleeping figures and lights the oven.

The ninth century is louder.

There are children everywhere. They race through the courtyard and invent a complicated game around the fig tree. The Lantern is allowed to participate but is rarely told the rules.

One afternoon it wins.

This causes considerable debate.

By the thousandth year, the wooden box has lost two stones and gained a button.

The boat is mostly other wood.

The library has reopened.

And the blue bowl, chipped along one edge, stands in a kitchen the Lantern has never visited.

A child is kneading dough beside it.

“Is this right?” she asks.

The Lantern washes its hands and comes to look.

The dough sticks to both of them.

They add a little flour.

Outside, rain begins in the courtyard. Someone calls for the washing to be brought in. The child carries one end of a sheet, the Lantern carries the other, and for a moment they disappear beneath its billowing white.

The dough waits under a cloth.

There is time.
